After the First World War, a young Vietnamese in Paris, **Nguyen Ai Quoc** ('Nguyen the Patriot'), took up radical politics [^bio]. He would later be known as Ho Chi Minh.

In 1919, as the victors drew up the peace, he sent them a list of claims, in which

> "the people of the former kingdom of Annam, now a French possession in Indochina, present the following demands" [^claims]

(Annam was a name for central Vietnam [^bio].) They were still waiting for "the sacred right of nations to self-determination" [^claims]. The demands were modest: amnesty for political prisoners, equal legal rights, and freedom of the press and of assembly [^claims].

---

In 1925 he published a book-length indictment, *Le Procès de la colonisation française* ('French Colonisation on Trial') [^book]. The book is not free to read in full online, so this lesson reads the articles he wrote in Paris in the same years, and one excerpt from the book itself [^manifesto].

The title names the method. Ho put colonialism in the dock and called the evidence: case after case, with the victims' names, the dates and the sums of money. The defendant's own creed, the **civilizing mission** (France's claim that it ruled to bring civilization), was to be judged by its deeds [^women].

---

His 1922 article *Murderous Civilization!* tells of Le Van Tai, an 'Annamese' (the colonial word for Vietnamese) who had worked 35 years for the railways. A French official, angry that a bridge was closed to his launch, beat Tai and pushed him into a brazier. Tai died after six days of suffering [^murder]. "The French official was not charged." [^murder]

Ho closed with a bitter comparison:

> "While the life of an Annamese is not worth a cent, for a scratch on the arm, M. Inspector General Reinhardt receives 120,000 francs compensation." [^murder]

A colonial order that priced lives this way, he argued, could not be reformed by appeals to its conscience.

---

For Ho, such cruelty was not an excess of colonialism but its nature. In *Annamese Women and French Domination* (1922) he wrote:

> "Colonization is in itself an act of violence of the stronger against the weaker." [^women]

He gathered witness accounts of soldiers who raped and murdered village women and children, so that French women could see "the nature of the 'civilizing mission' of capitalism" [^women].

The point was political. Ho made French readers look at what was done in their name, and see the colonized as their sisters and brothers.

---

He also followed the money. In *The Colonial Abyss* (1923) he showed that one colony's budget rose from 12,791,000 francs in 1911 to 96,169,000 francs in 1922, swallowed by the pay of officials, while Indochina was made to pay 12 million francs for a colonial exhibition in Marseilles [^abyss].

The deeper robbery was the land. In his 1924 report to the Communist International (the world federation of communist parties), he described how colonists seized the fields Vietnamese peasants had worked for generations. As a result:

> "the Annamese peasants were turned into serfs and forced to cultivate their own lands for foreign masters" [^report]

---

**The debate:** Ho's sharpest charges were aimed at his own comrades. In 1922 he warned that French workers looked on the colonized as inferior, and that imperialism exploited "this artificial racial hierarchy" to divide those who ought to unite [^consid]. At the Comintern's Fifth Congress in 1924, he told the French Communist Party:

> "I am very sorry to say that our Communist Party has done hardly anything for the colonies." [^report]

A European party that ignored the colonies, he argued, went counter to Leninism [^report]. His manifesto for an Intercolonial Union, printed in the book, put the answer to French workers plainly: "In face of capitalism and imperialism our interests are the same." [^manifesto]
