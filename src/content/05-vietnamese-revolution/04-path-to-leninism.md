After the First World War, Nguyen Ai Quoc, the future Ho Chi Minh, lived in Paris [^bio]. In 1960, for the ninetieth anniversary of Lenin's birth, he wrote for a Soviet review how he had found his way to Lenin [^path].

He made his living "now as a retoucher at a photographer's, now as painter of 'Chinese antiquities' (made in France!)" and handed out leaflets against French crimes in Vietnam [^path]. Of Russia's **October Revolution**, the workers' revolution Lenin led, Ho admits:

> "At that time, I supported the October Revolution only instinctively, not yet grasping all its historic importance." [^path]

He admired Lenin as a great patriot who freed his people, but had read none of his books [^path].

---

Ho joined the French Socialist Party because its members had shown sympathy for the oppressed peoples. Yet, he confesses, "I understood neither what was a party, a trade-union, nor what was socialism nor communism." [^path]

The branches were in a heated debate. Should the party stay in the **Second International** (the old federation of socialist parties), found a 'Second and a half' International, or join Lenin's **Third International**, the Communist International? Ho sat through the meetings two or three times a week, puzzled. What he most wanted to know was never debated:

> "which International sides with the peoples of colonial countries?" [^path]

When he asked, comrades answered: the Third [^path].

---

A comrade handed him Lenin's theses on the national and colonial questions, printed in *l'Humanité* (the party's daily) [^path]. Lenin argued that communists must do more than proclaim the brotherhood of nations:

> "a policy must be pursued that will achieve the closest alliance, with Soviet Russia, of all the national and colonial liberation movements" [^lenin]

Ho read it again and again until he grasped it. He was overjoyed to tears. Alone in his room, he shouted as if to a crowd: "This is what we need, this is the path to our liberation!" [^path]

From then on, he had entire confidence in Lenin and the Third International [^path].

---

The listener became a fighter. Still short of French words, Ho threw himself into the branch debates and defended Lenin against all comers [^path]. His "only argument" was:

> "If you do not condemn colonialism, if you do not side with the colonial people, what kind of revolution are you waging?" [^path]

He took his position to other branches too. In December 1920, at the party's congress in the city of Tours, he voted with the majority to join the Communist International [^gloss] [^path]. That made him one of the founders of the **French Communist Party** [^bio].

---

Here is the heart of the essay. Ho did not come to Lenin as a Marxist:

> "At first, patriotism, not yet communism, led me to have confidence in Lenin, in the Third International." [^path]

Step by step, studying Marxism-Leninism while taking part in practical work, he concluded that "only socialism and communism can liberate the oppressed nations and the working people throughout the world from slavery" [^path].

For a colonized people, national liberation and socialism were not rival goals. Ho came to see them as one road.

---

Ho ended with a legend known in Vietnam and China: a miraculous 'Book of the Wise' that shows the way out of any difficulty [^path]. Leninism, he wrote, was more than that:

> "it is also the radiant sun illuminating our path to final victory, to socialism and communism" [^path]

He had said much the same when Lenin died in 1924, writing in *Pravda* (the Soviet party's paper) that Lenin was "the bright star showing us the way to the socialist revolution" [^colonial]. The essay shows how a colonial patriot became a communist, and why he never stopped being a patriot.
