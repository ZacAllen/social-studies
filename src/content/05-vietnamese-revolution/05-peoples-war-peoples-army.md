**Võ Nguyên Giáp** (1911–2013) was a principal commander in the wars against France and the United States, and commander of the Vietnam People's Army [^mia]. In 1959, for the fifteenth anniversary of the People's Army, he wrote the article that gave this collection its name [^ch2].

His question: how did a colony with no army defeat France, backed by the United States? The French generals had expected an easy walkover. The Vietnamese army was then "weak in all respects", Giáp admits [^ch2]. His answer:

> "The Vietnamese people won because their war of liberation was a people's war." [^ch2]

The colonialists had alienated a whole nation, and the whole nation rose against them [^ch2].

---

A **people's war** is fought by the whole people for the people's own aims. Giáp named two: to win back national independence, and to overthrow the feudal landlord class and bring land to the peasants [^ch2].

In a colony where peasants were the vast majority, these were one fight:

> "a people's war is essentially a peasant's war under the leadership of the working class" [^ch1]

So the land question was decisive [^ch1]. The Party built a broad **national united front**, uniting every patriotic class, even progressive landlords, on the basis of the alliance of workers and peasants [^ch2].

---

The enemy was much stronger, so the war had to be long. Giáp warned:

> "All the conceptions born of impatience and aimed at obtaining speedy victory could only be gross errors." [^ch1]

The Vietnamese had to wear the enemy down little by little while building their own strength, until the balance of forces turned [^ch2]. Such a war passed through three stages: contention, equilibrium, and counter-offensive [^ch2]. Not every revolutionary war must be long, Giáp added; Vietnam's was, because of the balance of forces at its start [^ch2].

---

The form of fighting was **guerrilla war**, the war of a poor people against a modern army:

> "Is the enemy strong? One avoids him. Is he weak? One attacks him." [^ch2]

The front was wherever the enemy was. "Each inhabitant was a soldier, each village a fortress" [^ch2]. Weapons came from the battlefield, so the French Expeditionary Corps became, unwittingly, the People's Army's supplier [^ch2].

Step by step, guerrilla war grew into **mobile war** (larger units maneuvering in campaigns): from platoons and companies to regiments, then divisions, and finally Dien Bien Phu [^ch2].

---

What made this a new kind of army? It fought for the people's interests, and most of its fighters were peasants and workers. Political work, which taught every soldier what he fought for, was "the soul of the army" [^ch2].

Its fighters swore an oath to respect, help and defend the people, and never to take even "a needle or a bit of thread" [^ch2]. Giáp used an old saying:

> "The people are to the army what water is to fish, as the saying goes." [^ch2]

Strict discipline went with internal democracy: the more democracy in a unit, Giáp argued, the stronger its unity [^ch2].

---

Giáp also faced the Party's errors. He wrote that in the early years the peasant question had been "under-estimated in importance" [^ch3]. On the **land reform** in the North (1953–1956), he said:

> "Despite the errors which blemished its accomplishment, it was a correct line crowned with success" [^ch1]

He added that the errors came mainly after peace was restored in 1954 [^ch3]. They were grave: peasants falsely labeled landlords, Party members purged, and thousands executed [^moise]. The subject's survey, *From the August Revolution to 1975*, gives the historians' estimates.

---

In 1954 the People's Army destroyed the French camp at Dien Bien Phu, where the Expeditionary Corps lost 16,000 men [^ch2]. Giáp drew the lesson:

> "A weak and small nation and a people's army, once resolved to stand up, to unite together and to fight for independence and peace, will have the full power to defeat all aggressive forces" [^ch4]

The book appeared in English in 1961, as the war against the United States was beginning [^mia]. Its publishers offered it to peoples still facing imperialism, hoping they would find in it "further reasons for confidence and hope" [^fwd].
