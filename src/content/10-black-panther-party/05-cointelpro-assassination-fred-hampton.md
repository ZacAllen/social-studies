In August 1967 the FBI started **COINTELPRO** (short for counterintelligence program), a covert national program against groups it labeled "Black Nationalist Hate Groups". By September 1968 the Black Panther Party was among its targets [^court].

An FBI memorandum of 1968 set out its goals. The first was to "Prevent a coalition of militant black nationalist groups" [^court]. The second:

> "Prevent the rise of a messiah who could unify and electrify the militant nationalist movement" [^court]

Others were to stop such groups from gaining respectability and to prevent their long-range growth, especially among youth [^court]. Fred Hampton, chairman of the Illinois Panthers, was building exactly the kind of coalition the FBI feared.

---

The Chicago FBI moved quickly. Its key agent inside the Party was **William O'Neal**, a paid informant the FBI had first contacted while he sat in the Cook County Jail. O'Neal joined the Panthers the day their Chicago office opened, in November 1968, and soon became chief of security [^court].

Through O'Neal the FBI worked to divide the Panthers from their allies. When the Panthers discussed merging with the **Blackstone Rangers**, a local street gang, the FBI sent the gang's leader an anonymous letter claiming the Panthers had a "hit out" on him [^court]. O'Neal also pushed racist Panther cartoons, opening a rift with the white student radicals of Students for a Democratic Society [^court]. For this work he received several pay raises [^court].

---

Hampton knew the danger. In May 1969 he told a Chicago audience:

> "we have got to grasp onto our leaders and protect our leaders before they're taken away." [^protect]

Pressure mounted. The Cook County State's Attorney, **Edward Hanrahan**, had a Special Prosecutions Unit, and by April 1969 its main target was Black street gangs. Chicago police ransacked Panther headquarters in July and October. On November 13 a man close to the Party, Jake Winters, killed two police officers in a shootout and was himself killed [^court].

On November 19, O'Neal helped his FBI handler draw a floor plan of Hampton's apartment, marking the bedroom he shared with Deborah Johnson [^court]. The FBI showed it to Hanrahan's office [^court].

---

At 4:30 a.m. on December 4, 1969, fourteen police officers from Hanrahan's office arrived at 2337 West Monroe Street, armed with a machine gun and other weapons. They had moved the raid to dawn "so that the sleeping occupants in the apartment could be taken by surprise" [^court].

Hampton was shot in his bed and killed, at 21 [^court] [^mia]. **Mark Clark**, another Panther, was also killed, and four others were wounded [^court].

Hanrahan claimed a fierce shootout. The evidence said otherwise. A federal grand jury found that police fired 90 to 99 shots, and the Panthers one, yet it indicted none of the raiders [^plo]. An FBI ballistics expert found no sign of a shot fired from the bedroom where Hampton lay [^court].

---

Then came the cover-up. The seven survivors were charged with attempted murder, with bond set at $100,000 each [^court]. Hanrahan held press conferences, gave the *Chicago Tribune* photographs claiming to show Panther bullet holes, and staged a televised re-enactment by the raiders [^court].

The FBI rewarded its informant with a $300 bonus [^court]. In February 1970 the head of its Chicago office told the grand jury that the FBI had not known of illegal weapons in the apartment, and did not mention the floor plan. The same day, his office asked to keep paying O'Neal, crediting the floor plan because it "saved injury and possible death to police officers" [^court].

---

The survivors and the families of Hampton and Clark sued. The first trial lasted eighteen months, and the judge ended it with verdicts for every defendant [^court]. In 1979 the Seventh Circuit appeals court reversed him. It found substantial evidence of a conspiracy [^ploappeal], and that a purpose of the cover-up was:

> "to prevent the development of widespread sympathy for the BPP cause which might have arisen out of a full disclosure of the facts surrounding the raid and the deaths of Clark and Hampton" [^court]

The Panthers' lawyers, the People's Law Office, record that the city, the county and the FBI then paid a $1.85 million settlement [^ploappeal]. None of the police who carried out the raid ever went to jail [^mia].

---

Hampton was not the only victim. By the end of the 1960s, according to the Party's attorney, twenty-eight Panthers had been killed [^carson].

In 1976 a U.S. Senate committee chaired by Frank Church exposed COINTELPRO. It concluded that the program assumed the FBI "has a role in maintaining the existing social order, and that its efforts should be aimed toward combating those who threaten that order" [^lfj].

The lesson for the movement was the one Hampton had drawn himself. Months before his death, he had given a speech titled *You Can Murder a Liberator, but You Can't Murder Liberation* [^liberator].
