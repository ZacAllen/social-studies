Amílcar Cabral (1924–1973) worked in the early 1950s as an agronomist in **Portuguese Guinea** (today Guinea-Bissau). The job took him to every village, and from what he saw he built an analysis and a strategy [^gca].

In 1956 he and others founded the **PAIGC**, the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cabo Verde [^gca]. Its program called for total independence and for unity between the peoples of Guinea and the Cabo Verde Islands [^prog].

Portugal, under a fascist government, called its African colonies 'provinces of Portugal'. In 1961 Cabral counted 15 million people under its rule, 12 million of them African [^gcvpc].

---

In August 1959, dock workers and seamen struck in Bissau, the capital. On the Pijiguiti docks, Cabral later wrote, "in just 20 minutes the Portuguese shot to death 50 African workers and wounded more than 100" [^ppt]. His 1961 account spoke of "dozens" of strikers shot down [^gcvpc].

Cabral drew a hard lesson. The massacre "showed that our Party was following a mistaken line" [^tfv]: it had hoped to win through strikes in the towns.

A secret conference that September changed course. The party began preparing for armed struggle and sent its town members into the countryside to mobilize the peasants [^tfv].

---

Before fighting, the PAIGC studied the society it meant to free. Among the **Fulas**, chiefs ruled a semi-feudal order and had long been allied with the Portuguese. The **Balantes**, by contrast, had no social stratification, only village councils of elders [^bassg].

In the towns, Cabral found one group especially dynamic: young people recently arrived from the countryside, living off relatives. Many of the party's cadres came from them [^bassg]. Why?

> "The importance of this urban experience lies in the fact that it allows comparison: this is the key stimulant required for the awakening of consciousness." [^bassg]

---

The PAIGC began its war in 1963 [^ppt]. It started, Cabral said, with "a people with a 99% illiteracy rate" and "only 14 university-trained men" [^ppt].

By the end of 1968, he reported, the **liberated areas** covered about 60 percent of the country, where half the people lived [^tds]. There the party organized production, security, schools and health care [^tds]. Women led village committees and sat at every level of the party [^ppt].

In 1972 a **National People's Assembly** was created, chosen by popular vote in the liberated territories [^gca].

---

Cabral demanded honesty from the party. A 1965 directive admitted "many faults and errors in our action whether political or military" [^tnl], and reminded members of what the struggle was for:

> "Always bear in mind that the people are not fighting for ideas, for the things in anyone's head. They are fighting to win material benefits, to live better and in peace, to see their lives go forward, to guarantee the future of their children." [^tnl]

Fighters were to remember "that we are armed militants and not militarists" [^tnl]. And the rule for leaders: "Mask no difficulties, mistakes, failures. Claim no easy victories" [^tnl].

---

Cabral did not live to see victory. On January 20, 1973, he was assassinated in Conakry, in the neighboring Republic of Guinea, where the PAIGC had its headquarters [^gs]. His killers had help from Portuguese agents operating within the PAIGC [^gca]. The enemy had reached him through his own party.

The movement went on. On September 24, 1973, the PAIGC National Assembly, meeting at Boe, declared Guinea-Bissau independent. After Portugal's own revolution in April 1974, Lisbon recognized it that September [^gs].

Cabral's half-brother Luís became president. In late 1980 he was overthrown in a coup led by his prime minister, João Bernardo Vieira [^gs].

---

**The debate:** Fanon had written that "in the colonial countries the peasants alone are revolutionary, for they have nothing to lose and everything to gain" [^fanon1]. He also saw the uprooted urban poor as "one of the most spontaneous and the most radically revolutionary forces of a colonized people" [^fanon2].

Cabral, speaking in 1964 at a center named for Fanon, disagreed on both counts [^bassg]. The **déclassés** (beggars, prostitutes and others outside any class) had helped the Portuguese police [^bassg]. And of the peasants:

> "physically, the peasantry is a great force in Guinea … but we know from experience what trouble we had convincing the peasantry to fight." [^bassg]
