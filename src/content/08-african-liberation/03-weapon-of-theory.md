In January 1966, revolutionaries from three continents met in Havana for the first **Tricontinental Conference** of the peoples of Asia, Africa and Latin America [^wot].

Amílcar Cabral (1924–1973), an agronomist, had founded the **PAIGC** (African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cabo Verde) in 1956. Since 1963 it had waged armed struggle against Portuguese rule in what is now Guinea-Bissau [^gca].

Cabral would not spend his speech insulting the enemy. As an African saying puts it, "When your house is burning, it's no use beating the tom-toms." [^wot] The real shout against imperialism was "to take up arms and fight" [^wot].

---

Cabral raised a subject missing from the agenda: "the struggle against our own weaknesses" [^wot]. Revolution is not an export good, he argued. Each people's struggle is shaped by its own history and its own internal contradictions [^wot].

The movements' worst weakness was in their heads:

> "The ideological deficiency, not to say the total lack of ideology, within the national liberation movements … constitutes one of the greatest weaknesses of our struggle against imperialism, if not the greatest weakness of all." [^wot]

Every practice produces a theory, he reminded his audience, and "nobody has yet made a successful revolution without a revolutionary theory" [^wot].

---

Cabral then asked a hard question. If history is the history of classes, what about peoples who had no classes when imperialism arrived, such as the Balantes of Guinea? Were they "living without history" [^wot]? He refused that conclusion.

Classes themselves, he argued, grow out of the **productive forces** (tools, skills and techniques) and the pattern of ownership. So the deeper cause lies there:

> "the level of productive forces, the essential determining element in the content and form of class struggle, is the true and permanent motive force of history." [^wot]

History ran before classes, and it will go on after them: "man will outlive classes" [^wot].

---

Cabral defined imperialism as the search for profit by monopoly finance capital, "piracy transplanted from the seas to dry land" [^wot]. It rules directly, through foreign officials and settlers (**colonialism**), or indirectly, through native agents (**neocolonialism**) [^wot].

Either way, it does the same thing: it seizes the freedom of a people's productive forces to develop, and so stops its history. Liberation means taking that history back:

> "the national liberation of a people is the regaining of the historical personality of that people, its return to history through the destruction of the imperialist domination to which it was subjected." [^wot]

---

Liberation, Cabral warned, "does not finish at the moment when the national flag is raised and the national anthem played" [^wot]. Under neocolonialism, a local 'pseudo-bourgeoisie' serves foreign capital and "cannot be a national bourgeoisie" [^wot].

> "there are only two possible paths for an independent nation: to return to imperialist domination (neo-colonialism, capitalism, state capitalism), or to take the way of socialism." [^wot]

Because imperialism rules by violence, he concluded, "the normal way of national liberation, imposed on peoples by imperialist repression, is armed struggle" [^wot].

---

Who inherits the state after the colonizer leaves? Cabral's answer was the **petty bourgeoisie**: the educated layer of clerks, officials and professionals. It is the only group that knows how to run the inherited state, but it owns no means of production of its own [^wot].

So it faces a choice: grow into a new bourgeoisie, or take the harder road:

> "the revolutionary petty bourgeoisie must be capable of committing suicide as a class in order to be reborn as revolutionary workers, completely identified with the deepest aspirations of the people to which they belong." [^wot]

The dilemma, in short: "to betray the revolution or to commit suicide as a class" [^wot].

---

**The debate:** The *Communist Manifesto* (1848) had opened: "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." [^manifesto] In 1888 Engels added a note limiting it: "That is, all written history." [^manifesto] Little was then known of society before records.

Cabral, speaking from Guinea, pushed the revision further. Those who call class struggle the motive force of history are right, he said, but

> "if class struggle is the motive force of history, it is so only in a specific historical period." [^wot]

For him this made Marxism stronger: it gave classless peoples a place in history, and gave socialist societies a history after classes disappear [^wot].
