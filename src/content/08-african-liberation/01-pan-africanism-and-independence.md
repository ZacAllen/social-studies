From October 15 to 21, 1945, the **Fifth Pan-African Congress** met in Chorlton Town Hall, Manchester, England [^pac]. **Pan-Africanism** is the movement to unite Africans and people of African descent against colonial rule and racism.

The African American scholar W.E.B. Du Bois chaired the congress. But the Trinidad-born organizer George Padmore was, as Du Bois remembered, "the organising spirit of that congress" [^pac]. Among the delegates were Kwame Nkrumah of the Gold Coast, a British colony in West Africa, and Jomo Kenyatta of Kenya, both future heads of state [^pac].

The congress spoke to the colonial powers bluntly:

> "We are determined to be free. We want education. We want the right to earn a decent living" [^challenge]

---

The congress also issued a *Declaration to the Colonial Workers, Farmers and Intellectuals*. Its reasoning was simple: "The object of imperialist powers is to exploit." [^decl] Self-government would defeat that object, so the fight for political power came first [^decl].

The Declaration named who would win it. Workers and farmers had to organize, because "Colonial workers must be in the front of the battle against Imperialism." [^decl] Their weapons were the strike and the boycott, which it called "invincible" [^decl]. Educated colonials were told to join the organization of the masses [^decl].

It closed with a slogan: "Colonial and Subject Peoples of the World-Unite!" [^decl]

---

Nkrumah went home to the Gold Coast in 1947. In 1949 he founded the **Convention People's Party** (CPP). Its 'positive action' campaign of 1950 landed him in jail, but when the CPP swept the 1951 elections he was freed to form a government. In 1957 he led the colony to independence as **Ghana** [^gnk]. Padmore came to advise him [^gpad].

By 1961 he counted some 28 independent states in Africa [^isf]. He welcomed them, and worried:

> "It is heartening to see so many new flags hoisted in place of the old; it is disturbing to see so many countries of varying sizes and at different levels of development, weak and, in some cases, almost helpless." [^isf]

His answer was a political union of Africa [^isf].

---

France ruled **Algeria**, in North Africa, as part of France itself, a 'French land' by law [^procl]. On November 1, 1954, the **National Liberation Front** (FLN) proclaimed its goal of national independence and called Algerians to join it [^procl]. It promised:

> "It is true that the struggle will be long, but the result is certain." [^procl]

Led by Ahmed Ben Bella, one of its founders, and others, the FLN fought a long war of independence [^gben]. Frantz Fanon, a psychiatrist from Martinique working in Algeria, joined the movement in 1954 and became an editor of its paper, *El Moudjahid* [^gfanon].

Algeria won independence in 1962, with Ben Bella as its first prime minister [^gben].

---

The price was terrible, and its size is still disputed. Estimates of Algerian dead run from about 250,000 to 1.5 million [^perv]. Algeria's 1963 constitution honored "more than a million martyrs" [^rahal]. The French historians Xavier Yacono and Charles-Robert Ageron, comparing Algeria's censuses of 1954 and 1966, put the population loss below 300,000, and perhaps below 250,000 [^perv].

In 1974 Algeria's own Ministry of Former Moudjahidine (veterans) counted 152,863 fighters killed, out of 336,748 [^perv]. About 24,000 soldiers of the French forces died [^perv].

French forces also drove more than two million rural Algerians from their land into camps ringed with barbed wire [^perv].

---

Portugal held on longest. Its government treated Angola, Mozambique and Portuguese Guinea as integral parts of Portugal [^gsp]. Revolt began in Angola in 1961 [^gsp]. Amílcar Cabral's **PAIGC** (African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cabo Verde) began its war in Portuguese Guinea in 1963 [^gca]. On September 25, 1964, **FRELIMO** (the Mozambique Liberation Front) proclaimed armed insurrection: "Our fight must not cease before the total liquidation of Portuguese colonialism." [^frelimo]

By 1973 Portugal had some 216,000 men under arms, more than 150,000 of them in Africa [^gsp]. War spending averaged 22 percent of state expenditure [^ferraz].

On April 25, 1974, young officers of the Armed Forces Movement overthrew the Caetano regime in Lisbon. Guinea-Bissau won independence that September, Mozambique and Angola in 1975 [^gsp].

---

Independence brought new defeats. In 1965 General Houari Boumedienne deposed Ben Bella in a military coup [^gben]. In Ghana, Nkrumah made the country a one-party state in 1964, and in 1966 the military overthrew him, with Western backing [^gnk].

Why did freedom prove so fragile? The lessons that follow take up three answers. Fanon warned about the class that inherits the new state. Nkrumah named the system of control that outlives the flag. Cabral asked what theory a liberation movement needs, and showed it at work in the PAIGC's war.
