Frantz Fanon (1925–1961) was a psychiatrist from Martinique, a French colony in the Caribbean. From 1953 to 1956 he headed the psychiatry department of the Blida-Joinville Hospital in **Algeria**, then ruled as part of France [^gloss].

He joined the Algerian liberation movement in 1954, and in 1956 became an editor of its newspaper, *El Moudjahid* ('The Fighter'). In 1960 the rebels' provisional government made him ambassador to Ghana. *The Wretched of the Earth* appeared in 1961, shortly before his death [^gloss].

The book asked how the colonized could free themselves, and what could go wrong once the new flag was raised.

---

The first chapter, 'Concerning Violence', opens bluntly:

> "National liberation, national renaissance, the restoration of nationhood to the people, commonwealth: whatever may be the headings used or the new formulas introduced, decolonization is always a violent phenomenon." [^violence]

Why? Because "The colonial world is a world cut in two." [^violence] The settler's town and the native's town face each other across a line held by the army and the police.

For Fanon, decolonization is not a polite handover of offices. Its proof of success lies in "a whole social structure being changed from the bottom up" [^violence].

---

Fanon then asks what the shared struggle does to the colonized themselves. Colonialism kept people divided by region and tribe; fighting together made them one people [^violence]. It also changed each fighter:

> "At the level of individuals, violence is a cleansing force. It frees the native from his inferiority complex and from his despair and inaction; it makes him fearless and restores his self-respect." [^violence]

A people that has freed itself, he adds, sees "that the liberation has been the business of each and all and that the leader has no special merit" [^violence]. That warning about leaders runs through the rest of the book.

---

'The Pitfalls of National Consciousness' looks past independence. Fanon expected the new rulers to come from the **national bourgeoisie**: the local middle class of doctors, lawyers and traders that steps into the departing Europeans' shoes [^pitfalls].

This class, he wrote, is "not engaged in production, nor in invention" [^pitfalls]. Its job is to be a go-between:

> "its mission has nothing to do with transforming the nation; it consists, prosaically, of being the transmission line between the nation and a capitalism, rampant though camouflaged, which today puts on the masque of neocolonialism." [^pitfalls]

**Neocolonialism** is foreign control that carries on behind a flag of independence.

---

A class that cannot give the people bread and land must hold them in check another way. Fanon's answer was one party and one leader:

> "The single party is the modern form of the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie, unmasked, unpainted, unscrupulous and cynical." [^pitfalls]

The leader who once roused the people now soothes them with memories of the independence struggle. "The leader pacifies the people." [^pitfalls]

And the party, which should carry the people's voice upward, becomes "a screen between the masses and the leaders" [^pitfalls].

---

Fanon's remedy was **political education**, which aims "not to treat the masses as children but to make adults of them" [^pitfalls]. The people must learn

> "that there is no such thing as a demiurge, that there is no famous man who will take the responsibility for everything, but that the demiurge is the people themselves and the magic hands are finally only the hands of the people." [^pitfalls]

(A **demiurge** is a godlike maker of the world.) Even a bridge, he wrote, should be built so that it enriches "the awareness of those who work on it" [^pitfalls].

---

Fanon's closing point is that nationalism is a starting place. Unless it grows quickly into "a consciousness of social and political needs, in other words into humanism, it leads up a blind alley" [^pitfalls].

The book's last pages turn away from Europe, which had talked endlessly of Man while leaving behind "an avalanche of murders" [^conclusion]:

> "Let us decide not to imitate Europe; let us combine our muscles and our brains in a new direction." [^conclusion]

The goal was a new kind of person, the 'whole man' that Europe had failed to bring into being [^conclusion].
