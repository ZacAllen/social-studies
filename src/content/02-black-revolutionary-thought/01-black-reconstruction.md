---

When W.E.B. Du Bois published *Black Reconstruction in America* in 1935, the standard history, taught by the **Dunning School** at elite universities, told a racist fairy tale. In that telling, Reconstruction (1865–1877) was a disaster of corrupt carpetbaggers and ignorant freedpeople, ended only by the return of white rule.

Du Bois, a founder of the NAACP and by then a serious student of Marx, turned that story upside down [^br1]. He treated Reconstruction as one of the great democratic experiments in history, with Black workers as its central actors: not passive recipients of freedom, but the force that won it.

---

The book's boldest argument concerns the Civil War itself. Slaveholders boasted that their slaves had stayed loyal. Du Bois showed the opposite. As Union armies advanced, enslaved people left the plantations by the hundreds of thousands, taking their labor from the Confederacy to the North as laborers, spies and soldiers.

He named this in the language of the labor movement:

> "It was a general strike that involved directly in the end perhaps a half million people." [^br4]

In this telling, emancipation was not simply granted by Lincoln. Enslaved workers seized much of it themselves.

---

After the war, freedpeople allied with Radical Republicans in what Du Bois called **abolition-democracy**. Across the South, Black voters elected legislatures that wrote new constitutions and built the region's first public school systems.

Du Bois described this in Marxist terms. Backed by federal troops, "a dictatorship of labor was to be attempted": "one of the most extraordinary experiments of Marxism that the world, before the Russian revolution, had seen." [^br9]

The experiment's weak point was land. Freedpeople demanded land of their own, the promised *forty acres and a mule*, but without land they stayed dependent on the planters who had once owned them.

---

Why didn't poor white workers join Black workers against the planters and employers they both faced? Du Bois's answer became one of the most influential ideas in American radical thought:

> "It must be remembered that the white group of laborers, while they received a low wage, were compensated in part by a sort of public and psychological wage." [^br16]

White workers got deference, access to public places, better schools, and a police force drawn from their own ranks. "The result of this was that the wages of both classes could be kept low" [^br16]. Racism split the working class, and both halves paid for it.

---

In 1877 the North withdrew its troops and abandoned the freedpeople to terror, Black Codes and Jim Crow. Du Bois summed it up:

> "The slave went free; stood a brief moment in the sun; then moved back again toward slavery." [^br1]

**The debate:** The "wages of whiteness" is still argued over on the left. Historian David Roediger built it into a theory of how white identity was made. Adolph Reed Jr. argues that Du Bois was describing a ruling-class strategy in a specific time and place, not a permanent bribe that makes white workers unorganizable [^reed].
