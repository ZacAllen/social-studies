Revolution came to Upper Volta on the night of August 4, 1983 [^un]. A year later the country had a new name, Burkina Faso, and on October 4, 1984, its leader, Thomas Sankara, stood before the **General Assembly**, the forum of all the United Nations' member states [^un].

He spoke for a country of 274,000 square kilometers and 7 million people. He set out his purpose plainly:

> "I do not intend to enunciate dogmas here. I am neither a messiah nor a prophet. I possess no truths." [^un]

He would speak in the language of facts, for his people and for the disinherited masses of the **third world** (the poor nations of Asia, Africa and Latin America) [^un].

---

The 'third world', Sankara said, was a label the other worlds invented at independence to keep the poor nations alienated. He took it up only to affirm a solidarity uniting Asia, Latin America and Africa against "the same political traffickers and economic exploiters." [^un]

For 20 years the poor nations had been sold models of development by charlatans. He drew the lesson:

> "There can be no salvation for us unless we reject those models; there can be no development without that break." [^un]

He turned on Africa's educated elite, who brought home from European universities only their diplomas. Ideas were weapons, he warned, and "there is no innocent writing." [^un]

---

What had foreign aid done? Few countries had received as much as Upper Volta, yet there was no sign of development [^un]. The figures: infant mortality of 180 per 1,000, life expectancy of 40 years, an illiteracy rate of 98 percent, and one doctor for 50,000 inhabitants [^un].

Aid, he concluded, was only aid for survival, and it sped up the country's debts. "The source of the evil was political and so the only cure must be a political one." [^un] So:

> "We refuse simple survival." [^un]

The revolution would rebuild the administration, and put the army to productive work beside the people, because "without patriotic training, a soldier is only a criminal with power." [^un]

---

Then Sankara widened the circle. He spoke for the millions in ghettos because their skin is black, for the Indigenous peoples of the Americas confined to reservations, for the unemployed, and for women exploited by a system imposed by men [^un]. He spoke for mothers watching their children die of malaria and diarrhea, and for the Palestinians driven from their land [^un].

Why could the president of a small African country speak for all of them? Because, he said:

> "I am a man and nothing that is human is alien to me." [^un]

---

The Burkinabè revolution, Sankara said, wished to inherit all the revolutions of the world [^un]. From the American revolution it took a lesson in fighting colonial rule. To the Monroe Doctrine's "America for the Americans" he answered "Africa for the Africans; Burkina Faso for the Burkinabe" [^un]. The French Revolution taught the rights of man, and the October Revolution of 1917 "made possible the victory of the proletariat" [^un].

The poor nations were demanding a **new international economic order**, a fairer system of world trade and finance. That right, like all the rights of peoples, could be won "only through the struggle of the peoples." [^un] No power would ever give it out of generosity.

---

Sankara reminded the Assembly what Africa had paid for the world's wealth. Of the Atlantic slave trade, he said: "For every black man who came to the plantations, five died or were crippled." [^un]

> "It is our blood that nourished the rise of capitalism, that made possible our present condition of dependence and consolidated our underdevelopment." [^un]

He called for Nelson Mandela's release and for South Africa's apartheid regime to be expelled from the UN. He demanded an end to the great powers' right of veto: Africans too had died fighting Hitler, and "nothing can justify that right" [^un].

He ended: "Fatherland or death: we shall triumph." [^un]
