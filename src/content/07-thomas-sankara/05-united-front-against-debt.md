On July 29, 1987, Thomas Sankara of Burkina Faso addressed the summit of the **Organization of African Unity** (OAU), the body of Africa's independent states, in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [^debt].

He began by needling the heads of state who had not come. How many were ready to fly to Paris, London or Washington when called, he asked, but could not come to Addis Ababa [^debt]? Then he turned to the crisis crushing the continent: **debt**, the loans African states owed to Western governments and banks. It was, he said, "an important condition of our survival, as much as peace." [^debt]

---

Sankara began with where the debt came from:

> "Debt's origins come from colonialism's origins. Those who lend us money are those who colonized us." [^debt]

The same people who once managed Africa's states and economies had become its lenders. Sankara said that Africans "had no connections with this debt. Therefore we cannot pay for it." [^debt]

The colonizers, he said, had turned themselves into 'technical assistants', or rather "technical assassins." [^debt] In its current form, controlled by imperialism, debt was "a skillfully managed reconquest of Africa" that made each country a financial slave [^debt].

---

Repayment, Sankara said, was not a moral question of honor. The arithmetic was simple:

> "if we don't repay, lenders will not die. That is for sure. But if we repay, we are going to die." [^debt]

The lenders, he argued, had "gambled as if in a casino." [^debt] While they were winning, nobody talked of a crisis. Now that they were losing they demanded payment: "they played, they lost, that's the rule of the game, and life goes on." [^debt]

So Africa could not repay, because it had no means to do so and was not responsible for the debt [^debt].

---

The rich countries, Sankara said, owed Africa a debt of their own, "that is blood debt." [^debt] Everyone spoke of the Marshall Plan that rebuilt Europe after the Second World War. Nobody spoke of the African troops who had helped save Europe from Nazism [^debt].

The crisis itself was a class struggle. It grew as the masses refused to let wealth be concentrated in a few hands. Talk of the morality of repaying hid this [^debt]:

> "The rich and the poor do not have the same morality." [^debt]

---

A country that refused alone would be crushed. So Sankara proposed an 'Addis Ababa Club', a united front against debt to match the rich nations' Paris Club and Group of Seven [^debt]. The working people of Europe were not Africa's enemies: "Those who want to exploit Africa are those who exploit Europe, too." [^debt]

He made the stakes personal:

> "If Burkina Faso stands alone in refusing to pay, I will not be here for the next conference!" [^debt]

---

Sankara joined debt to arms: "each time an African country buys a weapon, it is against an African country." [^debt] He proposed that poor countries limit their arms, and that Africa build its own market: "produce in Africa, transform in Africa, consume in Africa." [^debt] His delegation, he pointed out, wore cotton grown and woven in Burkina Faso.

Less than three months later, on October 15, 1987, Sankara was assassinated in a coup led by his comrade Blaise Compaoré [^aj].
