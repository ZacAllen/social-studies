On 8 March 1987, International Women's Day, Thomas Sankara addressed a great gathering of women in Burkina Faso, where a revolution had begun on 4 August 1983 [^women]. He spoke in French, the colonial language, and apologized to the women who did not understand it [^women].

The revolution of 4 August, he said, had given the men of Burkina honor and dignity. But something crucial was missing [^women]:

> "The revolution's promise is already a reality for men. But for women, it is still merely a rumor." [^women]

And yet, he insisted, the future of the revolution depended on women [^women].

---

Where does women's subjugation come from? Sankara credited **dialectical materialism**, Marx and Engels's method, with showing that it lies in a general system of exploitation, not in nature [^women].

Following Engels, he argued that for millennia relations between the sexes had been based on collaboration. Inequality came with the transition to a new form of society:

> "Humankind first knew slavery with the advent of private property." [^women]

The man who mastered slaves and land became the woman's master too. Property passed from father to son, and the **patriarchal family** (ruled by the father) appeared. So the status of women would improve only with the end of the system that exploits them [^women].

---

Women share the misery of all the exploited. But Sankara refused to reduce their oppression to class alone. A woman faces "a specific oppression": relations of conflict and violence with men themselves [^women].

> "The man, however; no matter how oppressed he is, has another human being to oppress: his wife." [^women]

Male brutality, he said, was a "safety valve" for the oppressed man, who hopes to regain the dignity the system denies him. Politically conscious women must therefore wage "a war on two fronts" [^women].

He also criticized men who called themselves revolutionaries yet beat their wives for going to meetings at night [^women].

---

Sankara described a Burkinabè woman's life: from girlhood she serves, as cook, cleaner, farmer and saleswoman [^women].

> "She is first to work and last to rest." [^women]

He gave figures: women were 99 percent illiterate and 52 percent of the population [^women].

The old neo-colonial regimes had answered with a Ministry of Women, led by a woman. Sankara called this a bourgeois approach. Women's conditions came from the whole social, political and economic structure, and after several years "nothing had changed for them" [^women].

---

What had the revolution done? It founded the **Women's Union of Burkina** (UFB), and it worked to eliminate forced marriage, prostitution and female circumcision [^women]. Women were working on irrigation projects, planting trees and joining vaccination brigades [^women].

Now Sankara proposed a plan of action for women involving every ministry, each to be judged on how well it carried it out:

> "Conceiving a development project without women's participation is like using only four fingers when we have ten." [^women]

He called for nurseries, day-care centers and cafeterias, so that women could join in revolutionary action, and declared that men and women would share the housework [^women].

---

Men who cling to their control over women, Sankara warned, place themselves on the side of counterrevolution:

> "For the revolution cannot triumph without the genuine emancipation of women." [^women]

He told the women plainly that they were dominated "by both imperialism and by men", and that "in every male languishes the soul of a feudal lord" [^women]. Only the revolutionary transformation of society could free them, but no one would hand them that freedom. The struggle to liberate women, he said, was above all their own struggle to deepen the revolution [^women].
