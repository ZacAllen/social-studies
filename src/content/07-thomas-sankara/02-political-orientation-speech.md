On August 4, 1983, a revolution triumphed in Upper Volta, a former French colony [^dop]. Two months later, on October 2, Thomas Sankara, leader of the new **National Council of the Revolution** (NRC, often called by its French initials, CNR), read the *Political Orientation Speech* on the radio [^dop].

It was written by a committee of the groups in the council, with Valère Somé doing most of the writing; Sankara amended the drafts and recorded it that evening [^jaffre]. It became the theoretical foundation of the revolution [^jaffre]. Its opening questions:

> "In short: where have we come from? And where are we going?" [^dop]

---

The speech starts with the legacy. In 1960 France, beaten at Dien Bien Phu in Vietnam and in trouble in Algeria, granted Upper Volta its independence [^dop]. For the people that was a victory, but from the point of view of imperialism "it was only a change in the form of domination and exploitation of our people." [^dop]

> "Neo-colonial and colonial society are fundamentally no different." [^dop]

A colonial army, administration and school became neo-colonial ones with the same role, now run by local allies of imperialism. The results: the rural sector employed more than 90 percent of the work force, and there was one doctor for 48,000 inhabitants [^dop]. In a phrase, "paradise for some and hell for others." [^dop]

---

Who, then, are the revolution's enemies and friends? The speech answers with a class analysis [^dop].

The enemies are the bourgeoisie, in three parts: a **state bourgeoisie** that grew rich by using the state as a capitalist uses a factory, a commercial bourgeoisie tied to imperialism, and a middle bourgeoisie squeezed by it. With them stand the "retrograde forces" of the old feudal chiefs [^dop].

The people are the working class, "young and small" but revolutionary; the **petty bourgeoisie** (small traders, intellectuals, artisans), which "very often hesitates between the cause of the masses and that of imperialism"; the peasantry, by number the main force; and the **lumpenproletariat**, the jobless who can be bought by reaction [^dop].

---

Every revolution, the speech says, has its own character. This one was happening in a backward farming country, without a working class conscious of its historic mission [^dop]. So:

> "it is a democratic and popular revolution." [^dop]

It is **democratic** because its tasks are ending imperialist domination and clearing away the barriers that keep the country backward. It is **popular** because the masses themselves take full part, and build a new state machine in place of the old [^dop].

The speech is frank about the limits. While anti-imperialist, the revolution is still carried out "within the limits of bourgeois social and economic regime." [^dop] It is not yet socialism.

---

The revolution's popular character is that "it is the movement of the immense majority in favor of the immense majority." [^dop] That is why its first act was to call for **Committees for the Defense of the Revolution** (CDRs).

The CDRs, in villages, urban neighborhoods and workplaces, are the organs through which the people exercise power. Their members discuss the decisions of the council and the government, and explain them to the masses [^dop]:

> "The CDR must be schools for political education." [^dop]

The speech demands a new revolutionary morality: the model militant is "one with the masses", has faith in them, and listens to them instead of giving orders [^dop].

---

The revolution must change every sector, starting with the army. The old army served imperialism; the new one will join in production, raising livestock and building schools and clinics. Soldiers will receive political training so as to become conscious revolutionaries, "being among the people as a fish in water." [^dop]

Then women. Under the weight of tradition, the speech says, a woman suffers doubly: the suffering men share, and more besides [^dop]. Her emancipation is no charity:

> "Revolution and liberation of women go together." [^dop]

---

Last comes the economy: land reform, an end to the burdens on the peasants, and self-sufficiency in food, built from "many small achievements" rather than giant projects [^dop]. Abroad, the speech pledges support to the people of Namibia under SWAPO, the Sahrawi people and the Palestinians [^dop].

The speech gave the revolution its program, its class analysis and its name, the 'Democratic and Popular Revolution', an idea Somé brought to the draft [^jaffre]. It closes:

> "Homeland or death, we shall overcome!" [^dop]
