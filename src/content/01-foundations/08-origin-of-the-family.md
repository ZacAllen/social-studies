---

Engels called this book "the execution of a bequest" [^pref]. Marx had planned to present the findings of the American anthropologist Lewis Henry Morgan in the light of historical materialism, and Engels took up the task in 1884 [^pref].

Morgan's *Ancient Society* (1877) studied kinship and property among peoples without states, above all the Iroquois of North America [^pref] [^ch3]. Engels saw it as an independent confirmation of Marx's method: Morgan "had discovered afresh in America the materialistic conception of history discovered by Marx forty years ago" [^pref].

The question: where did the family, private property and the state come from, and are they eternal?

---

Morgan's key discovery was the **gens** (plural *gentes*): a kin group tracing descent from a common ancestor, which he found among the Iroquois, especially the Seneca, and also behind the clans of ancient Greece and Rome [^ch3]. Each gens elected its own sachem (peace leader) and war chief [^ch3].

Engels marveled at how such a society kept order:

> "No soldiers, no gendarmes or police, no nobles, kings, regents, prefects, or judges, no prisons, no lawsuits … and everything takes its orderly course." [^ch3]

Disputes were settled by the whole community concerned [^ch3]. Society ran itself without a separate apparatus of force.

---

What changed? In the Old World, the taming of animals and the breeding of herds created a new and lasting source of wealth [^ch2c]. Herds belonged to the men. But under **mother-right**, descent ran through the female line, so a man's children belonged to their mother's gens and could not inherit from him [^ch2c].

As wealth grew, men overturned that rule. Engels drew the conclusion:

> "The overthrow of mother-right was the world historical defeat of the female sex. The man took command in the home also; the woman was degraded and reduced to servitude" [^ch2c]

---

The **monogamous family** followed. It rested on the man's supremacy, and its express purpose was to produce children of undisputed paternity to inherit his property [^ch2d]. So the oppression of women and the rise of private property are bound together:

> "The first class opposition that appears in history coincides with the development of the antagonism between man and woman in monogamous marriage, and the first class oppression coincides with that of the female sex by the male." [^ch2d]

Engels concluded that "the first condition for the liberation of the wife is to bring the whole female sex back into public industry" [^ch2d], ending the family's role as the economic unit of society.

---

As private wealth piled up, society split into rich and poor, free and slave. The old kin-based constitution could not hold these classes together, and the **state** arose in its place [^ch9]:

> "The state is therefore by no means a power imposed on society from without … Rather, it is a product of society at a particular stage of development; it is the admission that this society has involved itself in insoluble self-contradiction and is cleft into irreconcilable antagonisms which it is powerless to exorcise." [^ch9]

The state appears to stand above society, but it is normally "the state of the most powerful, economically ruling class" [^ch9].

---

If the state arose with classes, it can end with them. Engels wrote that a society organizing production anew on "free and equal association of the producers" would put the state machinery where it belongs: "into the museum of antiquities, next to the spinning wheel and the bronze ax" [^ch9].

Engels did not treat Morgan as final. In 1891 he admitted that new research had shaken or disproved "some of Morgan's minor hypotheses" [^pref4], while holding that the great leading ideas stood. The lasting point is the method: family, property and state have a history, and what has a history can change.
