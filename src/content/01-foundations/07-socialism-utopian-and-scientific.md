Around 1875, Eugen Dühring, a lecturer at Berlin University, announced his conversion to socialism. The German socialists had just united at Gotha, and Dühring began to build a sect around himself inside the young party [^intro]. Engels answered him in a long series of articles, later a book.

At the request of the French socialist Paul Lafargue, Engels arranged three chapters as a pamphlet. Lafargue translated it and published it in 1880 [^intro]. Its argument is in its title: socialism had to move from dreams to science. As Part I ends:

> "To make a science of Socialism, it had first to be placed upon a real basis." [^p1]

---

Engels began with the **utopian socialists** of the early 1800s: Saint-Simon, a son of the French Revolution; Fourier; and the British reformer Robert Owen [^p1]. Owen ran the great cotton mill at New Lanark, Scotland, from 1800 to 1829. He turned a demoralized population of up to 2,500 into a model community with no drunkenness, police or poor laws [^p1].

But they shared a limit:

> "One thing is common to all three. Not one of them appears as a representative of the interests of that proletariat which historical development had, in the meantime, produced." [^p1]

They hoped to free all humanity at once, by the power of reason and eternal justice [^p1].

---

Why did such gifted minds fail? Engels's answer was historical. When they wrote, capitalist production, and with it the conflict between bourgeoisie and proletariat, was still very incompletely developed [^p1].

So the utopians could not find the solution in society's real movement. They had to invent a perfect system in their heads, and impose it on society from without by propaganda and model experiments [^p1]:

> "These new social systems were foredoomed as Utopian; the more completely they were worked out in detail, the more they could not avoid drifting off into pure phantasies." [^p1]

---

The way forward came through **dialectics**: seeing the world not as fixed things but as processes of change and development. The old **metaphysical** way of thinking, Engels wrote, studied things one after the other, "fixed, rigid, given once for all" [^p2]. The philosopher Hegel first pictured the whole world as a process of constant motion and development [^p2]. "Nature is the proof of dialectics" [^p2], Engels wrote.

Marx put this method on a materialist footing:

> "These two great discoveries, the materialistic conception of history and the revelation of the secret of capitalistic production through surplus-value, we owe to Marx. With these discoveries, Socialism became a science." [^p2]

---

Part III states the **materialist conception of history** in Engels's words:

> "the final causes of all social changes and political revolutions are to be sought, not in men's brains, not in men's better insights into eternal truth and justice, but in changes in the modes of production and exchange." [^p3]

Under capitalism, production has become social: many workers combine in big factories to make one product. But the capitalist still owns that product alone. "The contradiction between socialized production and capitalistic appropriation manifested itself as the antagonism of proletariat and bourgeoisie." [^p3] It also bursts out in crises: five since 1825, Engels counted in 1877, with a sixth under way [^p3].

---

The solution follows from the contradiction: "The proletariat seizes political power and turns the means of production into State property." [^p3] In doing so it abolishes classes, and with them the need for a state to hold one class down.

Government of persons gives way to "the administration of things" [^p3]:

> "The State is not 'abolished'. It dies out." [^p3]

Planned production then lets humanity, for the first time, make its own history consciously. Engels called this "the ascent of man from the kingdom of necessity to the kingdom of freedom" [^p3]. Utopia was no longer a dream, but the outcome of a real struggle.
