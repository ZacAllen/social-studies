In 1847, Marx gave a series of lectures on economics to the German Workingmen's Club of Brussels. After the revolutions of 1848 were beaten back, he published them as articles in the *Neue Rheinische Zeitung* ('New Rhenish Gazette'), starting on April 4, 1849. The series stopped when the paper was suppressed that May [^intro].

Having traced the year's political battles, Marx turned to the economic conditions underneath them: the rule of the capitalist class and "the slavery of the workers" [^ch1]. He would take nothing for granted:

> "We wish to be understood by the workers." [^ch1]

---

Ask workers what they earn, Marx began, and they name a sum of money for a day or a task. It seems they sell their labor. "But this is merely an illusion." [^ch2] What they sell is **labor-power**, their capacity to work for a set time. (In 1891 Engels revised the text for a mass edition to make this distinction exact, as Marx had in his later work [^intro].)

The weaver is paid long before the cloth is sold. The capitalist buys his labor-power out of money already on hand, as he buys yarn and a loom:

> "Wages, therefore, are not a share of the worker in the commodities produced by himself." [^ch2]

---

For the worker, work is not living. It is the price of living:

> "He works that he may keep alive. He does not count the labour itself as a part of his life; it is rather a sacrifice of his life." [^ch2]

The slave was sold once and for all. The serf handed a share of his harvest to the lord. "The free labourer, on the other hand, sells his very self, and that by fractions" [^ch2], eight, ten or twelve hours a day. He can quit one employer, but he cannot escape them all: "He does not belong to this or that capitalist, but to the capitalist class" [^ch2].

---

What, then, is **capital**? The economists said: tools, raw materials and stored-up labor used to produce more. Marx answered that a thing is capital only within certain social relations. "A cotton-spinning machine is a machine for spinning cotton. Only under certain conditions does it become capital." [^ch5]

"Capital also is a social relation of production." [^ch5] It needs a class that owns nothing but its ability to work:

> "Capital does not consist in the fact that accumulated labour serves living labour as a means for new production. It consists in the fact that living labour serves accumulated labour as the means of preserving and multiplying its exchange value." [^ch5]

---

Do workers gain when capital grows fast? Wages may rise, Marx granted. But needs are social, measured against what others have:

> "A house may be large or small; as long as the neighboring houses are likewise small, it satisfies all social requirement for a residence. But let there arise next to the little house a palace, and the little house shrinks to a hut." [^ch6]

If wages rise 5 percent while profits rise 30, the worker falls further behind [^ch8]. The material position improves, Marx wrote, "but at the cost of his social position" [^ch8]. The workers forge "the golden chains by which the bourgeoisie drags it in its train" [^ch8].

---

Competition drives each capitalist to cut costs with more machinery and finer division of labor. Skilled work becomes simple, wages fall, and small owners are pushed down into the working class [^ch9]:

> "Thus the forest of outstretched arms, begging for work, grows ever thicker" [^ch9]

As production grows, so do the crises, which become "more frequent and more violent" [^ch9].

The lesson of these lectures: the wage system itself sets the interests of capital and labor against each other, and good times do not end that conflict.
