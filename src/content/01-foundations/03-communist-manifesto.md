In November 1847, a small workers' society called the **Communist League**, which under the conditions of the time had to be secret, held a congress in London. It commissioned two members, Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, to write "a detailed theoretical and practical programme for the Party" [^pref].

The manuscript went to the printer in London a few weeks before revolution broke out in Paris in February 1848 [^pref]. It opened by turning the rulers' fear into a challenge:

> "A spectre is haunting Europe … All the powers of old Europe have entered into a holy alliance to exorcise this spectre" [^ch1]

---

The first section, *Bourgeois and Proletarians*, begins with surprising praise. The **bourgeoisie** is the class that owns the factories, banks and trade, and "The bourgeoisie, historically, has played a most revolutionary part." [^ch1]

It tore up the old feudal ties of loyalty and custom. It cannot survive without constantly revolutionizing how things are made, so nothing stays fixed for long:

> "All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned, and man is at last compelled to face with sober senses his real conditions of life, and his relations with his kind." [^ch1]

Its hunger for markets "chases the bourgeoisie over the entire surface of the globe" [^ch1].

---

Yet the bourgeoisie has called up powers it cannot control. Every few years a **crisis** breaks out, which the *Manifesto* calls "the epidemic of over-production" [^ch1]: more goods than the market can absorb, so that products, and even whole productive forces, are destroyed.

Meanwhile modern industry crowds workers into great factories, where they learn to combine. The **proletariat** (wage workers, who own no means of production and must sell their labor power to live) grows in numbers and organization. So:

> "What the bourgeoisie therefore produces, above all, are its own grave-diggers." [^ch1]

---

The second section, *Proletarians and Communists*, says what communists want. They are not a sect apart: they "do not form a separate party opposed to the other working-class parties" [^ch2].

Their aim, in one line: "the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property." [^ch2] They mean bourgeois property, the ownership of the means of production, not a worker's belongings:

> "Communism deprives no man of the power to appropriate the products of society; all that it does is to deprive him of the power to subjugate the labour of others by means of such appropriations." [^ch2]

---

How would the change come? "the first step in the revolution by the working class is to raise the proletariat to the position of ruling class, to win the battle of democracy." [^ch2]

The workers in power would take capital from the bourgeoisie step by step. The *Manifesto* listed ten measures, among them a heavy progressive income tax, a national bank, and free education for all children in public schools [^ch2].

As classes disappear, so does the rule of one class over another. In place of the old society:

> "we shall have an association, in which the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all." [^ch2]

---

The *Manifesto* closes with its most famous lines: "The proletarians have nothing to lose but their chains. They have a world to win. Working Men of All Countries, Unite!" [^ch4]

Marx and Engels never treated it as scripture. In 1872 they wrote that its general principles still held, but some practical details were dated. The **Paris Commune** of 1871, when the city's workers held power for two months [^pref], had taught them something new:

> "the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery, and wield it for its own purposes." [^pref]
