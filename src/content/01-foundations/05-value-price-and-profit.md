In 1865, the European continent saw "a real epidemic of strikes, and a general clamour for a rise of wages" [^s1]. At the General Council of the **International Working Men's Association** (the First International), a carpenter and old follower of the socialist Robert Owen, John Weston, argued that a general rise in wages would be of no use to workers, and that trade unions did harm [^intro].

Marx answered at two sessions, on June 20 and 27, 1865 [^intro]. He honored Weston's courage, but called his theses, in their present form:

> "theoretically false and practically dangerous" [^s1]

The address was published only after Engels's death, by Marx's daughter Eleanor [^intro].

---

Weston's argument assumed that national production is a fixed pie. If workers took a bigger slice in wages, prices would simply rise and cancel the gain [^s1].

Marx showed that the premise was false: "The amount or magnitude of national production changes continuously." [^s1] Nor do wages set prices. When wages rise across the board, it is the capitalists' share that shrinks. His first resolution read:

> "A general rise in the rate of wages would result in a fall of the general rate of profit, but, broadly speaking, not affect the prices of commodities." [^s3]

So the fight over wages is a real fight over who gets what.

---

To prove it, Marx had to explain what value is. Supply and demand explain why prices swing up and down. They cannot explain the level around which they swing [^s1] [^s2].

What do a quarter of wheat and an ounce of gold have in common, so that they can be equal in exchange? Both took a certain amount of average human labor to produce [^s2]:

> "A commodity has a value, because it is a crystallization of social labour." [^s2]

The more labor a commodity needs, the more value it has.

---

What, then, does the worker sell? "What the working man sells is not directly his labour, but his labouring power, the temporary disposal of which he makes over to the capitalist." [^s2] The value of **labouring power** is what it takes to keep the worker going.

Say that takes six hours of labor a day. The capitalist pays for a day's labouring power, then has the spinner work twelve hours. The other six hours are **surplus labour**, and their product, **surplus value**, goes to the capitalist [^s2]. The wage form hides this:

> "On the basis of the wages system even the unpaid labour seems to be paid labour." [^s2]

---

So profit needs no cheating. The capitalist sells the yarn at its full value, including the unpaid hours: "normal and average profits are made by selling commodities not above, but at their real values." [^s2]

How big is that profit? Between the lowest possible wage and the longest possible working day there is a wide range. Where the line falls is settled by the continuous struggle between capital and labor:

> "The matter resolves itself into a question of the respective powers of the combatants." [^s3]

Even the limit on the working day, Marx noted, was never won in England without legislation forced by workers' pressure [^s3].

---

So Weston was wrong: unions matter. "Trades Unions work well as centers of resistance against the encroachments of capital." [^s3] Workers who gave way in everyday battles would disqualify themselves from any larger movement [^s3].

But wage fights alone treat effects, not causes. They slow the downward pressure without changing its direction [^s3]. Marx closed with a challenge:

> "Instead of the conservative motto: 'A fair day's wage for a fair day's work!' they ought to inscribe on their banner the revolutionary watchword: 'Abolition of the wages system!'" [^s3]
