By 1901, Russian socialism was a scatter of small circles hunted by the Tsar's police. A popular trend called **Economism** argued that workers should stick to the economic fight over wages and hours, and leave politics to the liberals.

Lenin, then an editor of the émigré newspaper *Iskra* ('The Spark'), answered with *What Is to Be Done?* (1902). He borrowed the title from Chernyshevsky's 1863 revolutionary novel. His starting principle:

> "Without revolutionary theory there can be no revolutionary movement." [^ch1]

(In Lenin's day, 'Social-Democrat' meant a Marxist revolutionary.)

---

Lenin studied Russia's strike waves of the 1890s. Even the early riots and machine-breaking showed something stirring: "consciousness in an embryonic form" [^ch2]. But an embryo is not yet grown.

> "The history of all countries shows that the working class, exclusively by its own effort, is able to develop only trade union consciousness" [^ch2]

That is the conviction that workers must form unions, fight the employers and press the government for labor laws. It is necessary, but it is not socialism. Lenin attacked the Economists' **worship of spontaneity**: trailing behind whatever workers were already doing instead of leading.

---

Where, then, does socialist consciousness come from? Lenin answered: "Class political consciousness can be brought to the workers only from without" [^ch3]. It comes from outside the narrow fight between workers and their own employers, by exposing how *every* class stands in relation to the state.

So his model organizer was not a union official:

> "the Social-Democrat's ideal should not be the trade union secretary, but the tribune of the people" [^ch3]

The tribune speaks out against every outrage, whether against students, religious sects, peasants or soldiers, and shows workers that the same forces are crushing them too.

---

Under the Tsar, open organizing meant arrest and exile, and amateur circles were broken up by the police within months. Lenin called for a disciplined, secret organization of **professional revolutionaries**: people who made revolution their full-time work and mastered the craft of evading the political police.

Recalling the amateurism of his own early circle, he wrote that with real organization they might have said:

> "Give us an organisation of revolutionaries, and we will overturn Russia" [^ch4]

This was no plan to replace the workers. He wanted the workers themselves to "advance" more and more such revolutionaries from their own ranks [^ch4].

---

How could one organization span a vast empire? Lenin's plan was an all-Russian political newspaper. Writing, smuggling and distributing it would bind scattered local groups into a single network with a common line. He repeated a line from his 1901 article *Where to Begin*:

> "A newspaper is not only a collective propagandist and a collective agitator, it is also a collective organiser." [^ch5]

He compared the paper to the scaffolding around a building under construction: it marks the outline of the structure and lets the builders coordinate their work. *Iskra* was already doing that job.

---

**The debate:** The party split in 1903 into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks. The Polish Marxist Rosa Luxemburg then attacked Lenin's organizational ideas, restated in his *One Step Forward, Two Steps Back* (1904), as "ultra-centralism". The working class, she argued, must learn through its own struggles:

> "Historically, the errors committed by a truly revolutionary movement are infinitely more fruitful than the infallibility of the cleverest Central Committee." [^lux]

Lenin answered that critics tore the book from its setting. In 1907 he called it "a controversial correction of Economist distortions" [^p1907], written for an illegal movement under the Tsar, not a timeless blueprint.
