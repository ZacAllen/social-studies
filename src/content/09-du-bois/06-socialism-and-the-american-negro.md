On April 9, 1960, W.E.B. Du Bois, then 92, spoke in the Great Hall of the Memorial Union in Madison, Wisconsin, at the invitation of the Wisconsin Socialist Club [^fw]. For a quarter century he had edited *The Crisis*, the NAACP's monthly magazine.

He opened with the state of American democracy:

> "Democracy has so disappeared in the United States that there are some subjects that cannot even be discussed." [^fw]

"Today Communism is the dirty word and Socialism is suspect." In Wisconsin itself, he noted, "Senator McCarthy succeeded in making America afraid to discuss Socialism" [^fw]. ('Negro' was the respectful term of the day.)

---

Du Bois retold his own road. The Niagara Movement of 1905 and then the NAACP fought for the vote and against lynching, and the legal fight culminated in the Supreme Court's 1954 decision against segregation [^fw].

"But during all this struggle we knew that something still was lacking." Black Americans remained poorer, sicker and less schooled than their white neighbors [^fw].

He first looked to **consumers' cooperation**: Black Americans organizing as buyers to create jobs and savings. But, he found, "without the power of the government it would fail." And "with government cooperation it was socialism." [^fw]

---

Du Bois had been barred from travel abroad from 1950 to 1958. In 1958 he set out on an eleven-month journey through Western Europe, Eastern Europe, the Soviet Union and China [^fw].

He left thinking capitalism could still be made progressive, as Franklin Roosevelt had shown. "I returned with completely changed ideas." [^fw]

In Western Europe he saw prosperity built on colonies, and a home working class bought off: "that laboring class was being bribed by political power and high wage" [^fw]. In the socialist countries he saw the masses treated as the owners of the nation. He drew a large conclusion:

> "In my mind there is no doubt but that the world of the 21st century will be overwhelmingly communistic." [^fw]

---

In China Du Bois believed he saw "willing cooperation and of a wide spread content and happiness which I had not seen elsewhere in the world." He admitted, "After all I saw these countries only partially and for brief visits" [^fw]. He celebrated his 91st birthday in Beijing in 1959 [^china].

That was the **Great Leap Forward**, launched in 1958 with the rural people's communes. In 1981 the Communist Party of China judged that 'Left' errors, "excessive targets, the issuing of arbitrary directions, boastfulness," along with natural disasters and cancelled Soviet contracts, caused "serious losses to our country and people" in 1959–1961 [^cpc].

Demographers estimate the famine's excess deaths at 16.5 to 30 million, and unpublished Chinese materials suggest close to 40 million [^smil].

---

Back home, Du Bois judged the civil rights fight by the same test. The NAACP's legal fight "has been an astonishing success but its very success shows the limitations of law and law enforcement." [^fw]

He hailed the Montgomery boycott and the student uprisings. "But it still does not reach the center of the problem." That center was "the chance for American Negroes to have money to spend because of employment by which they can make a decent wage." [^fw]

The next step, he said, was for Black Americans to insist on their legal rights and add "increasingly a socialistic form of government", meaning a welfare state [^fw]. "Does capitalism offer such a program? It does not. It offers war." [^fw]

---

Du Bois did not ask for "a compulsory rush into socialism." He asked for the right to learn about it. Those who disagreed, he said, had "no right to prevent the truth about socialism from being told." [^fw]

Above all, "American Negroes must know what is going on in the world today," so that they might rid themselves of poverty, ignorance and disease [^fw].

Eighteen months later, on October 1, 1961, Du Bois applied to join the Communist Party of the United States [^cp61]. The speech in Madison is the argument behind that decision: civil rights won in court needed an economic foundation, and Du Bois had concluded that only socialism could lay it.
