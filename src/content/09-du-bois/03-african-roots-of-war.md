In May 1915, with the First World War under way, the Black American scholar W.E.B. Du Bois published *The African Roots of War* in *The Atlantic Monthly*. Most readers assumed, he wrote, that Africa lay far from the war's causes [^arw].

He disagreed:

> "Yet in a very real sense Africa is a prime cause of this terrible overturning of civilization which we have lived to see." [^arw]

The usual story blamed the **Balkans**, the small, contested states of southeastern Europe. Du Bois called them "convenient for occasions," but held that "the ownership of materials and men in the darker world is the real prize that is setting the nations of Europe at each other's throats to-day." [^arw]

---

Du Bois traced the race for Africa. The **Berlin Conference**, which met on November 15, 1884, was called "to apportion the rising riches of Africa among the white peoples." Europe claimed a tenth of Africa in 1875; within twenty-five more years it had taken practically all of it [^arw].

He did not soften how it was done:

> "Lying treaties, rivers of rum, murder, assassination, mutilation, rape, and torture have marked the progress of Englishman, German, Frenchman, and Belgian on the dark continent." [^arw]

King Leopold of Belgium's Congo Free State, with its murder and mutilation, differed from the rest, he argued, "only in degree and concentration." [^arw]

---

Why the sudden hunger for colonies? Du Bois found the answer in Europe's own politics. At home, workers were winning political democracy and beginning to demand their share of wealth. As the chance of the 'Big Fortune' from exploiting workers at home waned, the dream of exploitation abroad grew [^arw].

So the white worker was invited to share the spoils of empire. Du Bois called this a **democratic despotism**: democracy at home, despotism abroad [^arw].

> "It is no longer simply the merchant prince, or the aristocratic monopoly, or even the employing class, that is exploiting the world: it is the nation; a new democratic nation composed of united capital and labor." [^arw]

---

Where did this new wealth come from? "It comes primarily from the darker nations of the world," Du Bois answered: Asia and Africa, Latin America, the West Indies and the South Seas [^arw].

To keep that wealth flowing, Europe built a doctrine. Science and religion were enlisted to teach "the natural inferiority of most men to the few." Racism was no accident of ignorance; it was an investment:

> "Thus, the world began to invest in color prejudice. The 'Color Line' began to pay dividends." [^arw]

Only the "aristocracy of labor," the better-paid workers, were let into the share of the spoils. The unskilled stayed outside, restless and increasingly revolutionary [^arw].

---

What, then, would make peace? Not European disarmament alone, Du Bois argued, which would only settle the division of the spoils. "We must extend the democratic ideal to the yellow, brown, and black peoples." [^arw]

He named three demands for Africa: land, modern education and **home rule** (self-government): "The ruling of one people for another people's whim or gain must stop." [^arw]

And he warned what would follow if not:

> "Then they are going to fight and the War of the Color Line will outdo in savage inhumanity any war this world has yet seen." [^arw]

---

A year after Du Bois wrote, Lenin, the Russian Marxist, wrote *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1916). In a 1920 preface he summed it up: the war was "a war for the division of the world, for the partition and repartition of colonies" [^lenin].

Lenin explained the split in the workers' movement the same way Du Bois had. Colonial superprofits made it "possible to bribe the labour leaders and the upper stratum of the labour aristocracy." [^lenin]

The two came at it from opposite ends. Lenin began from finance capital in Europe; Du Bois began from Africa and the color line [^arw]. Both concluded that empire, not a Balkan quarrel, made the war.
