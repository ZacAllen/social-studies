In 1911 W.E.B. Du Bois, editor of the NAACP's magazine *The Crisis*, joined the **Socialist Party of America** [^cp61]. Two years later he wrote to his new comrades in *The New Review*, a weekly review of international socialism, on the **Negro problem** (the standard term of the day for the oppression of Black Americans) [^snp].

His readers were socialists who argued from theory about where industry was heading. His thesis was blunt:

> "In the Negro problem, as it presents itself in the United States, theoretical Socialism of the twentieth century meets a critical dilemma." [^snp]

---

Du Bois began from what he saw in the party. No recent convention had "dared to face fairly the Negro problem" and declare Black people equal [^snp].

Instead, he wrote, thinking members held that socialists must not turn aside from their great objects for this issue: "let the question wait; when the objects of Socialism are achieved, this problem will be settled along with other problems." [^snp]

He saw a logical flaw. A group of ten million people could not be 'aside' from any program of socialism. His questions came in a run: "Can the objects of Socialism be achieved so long as the Negro is neglected? Can any great human problem 'wait'?" [^snp]

---

Du Bois put the choice in numbers. On one side were 90 million white Americans; on the other, 10 million Black Americans, mostly workers [^snp].

Many socialists, he argued, planned to end private control of capital and share the social income among the 90 million, while leaving the 10 million exploited. They assumed the majority would later share freely:

> "The idea is that ultimately when the 90,000,000 come to their own, they will voluntarily share with the 10,000,000 serfs." [^snp]

Did history justify that hope? "Frankly, I do not believe it does." Such a program, he wrote, was the old **industrial aristocracy** (rule by a privileged layer) with a bigger inner circle [^snp].

---

Against it Du Bois set a principle. In 1913 **Social Democracy** still meant socialism itself, and its essence, he wrote, was "that there shall be no excluded or exploited classes in the Socialistic state" [^snp].

From this came his measure of every movement:

> "I have come to believe that the test of any great movement toward social reform is the Excluded Class." [^snp]

Who does a reform not propose to benefit? He pressed the point home: "If you are saving dying babies, whose babies are you going to let die?" [^snp]

---

Du Bois also argued from strategy. By his count, one American laborer in five was Black. A socialism that shut them out would hand its enemies "the powerful weapon of 4,500,000 men" who would underbid wages and vote against labor laws [^snp].

The party's real temptation lay in the South. There the region's radical movement carried "a most bitter and reactionary hatred of the Negro." Courting it meant accepting that hatred [^snp]. He ended with a question to the party's leaders:

> "After you have gotten the radical South and paid the price which they demand, will the result be Socialism?" [^snp]

Du Bois soon left the party to back Woodrow Wilson [^cp61].

---

**The debate:** The socialist leader Eugene Debs was a fierce enemy of racism. He called the history of Black Americans "a history of crime without a parallel" [^debs].

But Debs held that "there is no Negro question outside of the labor question" and that the party should say only that the class struggle is colorless. "We have nothing special to offer the Negro, and we cannot make separate appeals to all the races." [^debs]

Du Bois's essay answered that view with a question: "If Socialism is going to settle the American problem of race prejudice without direct attack along these lines by Socialists, why is it necessary for Socialists to fight along other lines?" [^snp]
