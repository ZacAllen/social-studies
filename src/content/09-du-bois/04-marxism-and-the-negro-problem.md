In May 1933, in the depths of the Great Depression, W.E.B. Du Bois used *The Crisis*, the NAACP magazine he edited, to ask what Karl Marx meant for Black Americans. ('Negro' was the respectful term of the day.) [^mnp]

He began by insisting that Marx be taken seriously:

> "There are certain books in the world which every searcher for truth must know: the Bible, the Critique of Pure Reason, the Origin of Species, and Karl Marx' 'Capital.'" [^mnp]

Until the Russian Revolution, he noted, American universities had treated Marx condescendingly. Now Du Bois saw in him "a colossal genius of infinite sacrifice and monumental industry." [^mnp]

---

Du Bois laid out Marx's core argument for his readers. The capitalist owns the machines and materials; the worker owns little more than the ability to work. The worker's labor produces more than the worker is paid [^mnp].

That difference is **surplus value**, the source of profit. In Du Bois's words, it "represents, therefore, exploitation of the laborer," and it causes poverty, industrial crises and, in the end, social revolution [^mnp].

Behind the worn-out slogans, he wrote, there was still "living and insistent truth": "The class struggle of exploiter and exploited is a reality." [^mnp]

---

What had this to do with Black Americans? First, Du Bois answered, the mass of Black Americans "belong distinctly to the working proletariat" (the class that lives by wages). Fewer than 150 in every 1,000 Black workers could be counted bourgeois [^mnp].

But "this black proletariat is not a part of the white proletariat." The "lowest and most fatal degree" of its suffering came "not from the capitalists but from fellow white laborers":

> "It is white labor that deprives the Negro of his right to vote, denies him education, denies him affiliation with trade unions, expels him from decent houses and neighborhoods, and heaps upon him the public insults of open color discrimination." [^mnp]

---

Why had white workers turned on Black ones? Du Bois saw something Marx had not. A layer of engineers, managers and better-paid workers with savings had become a new **petty bourgeoisie** (small owners), tied to capital [^mnp].

Common white labor, meanwhile, was "blinded by the American vision" of climbing into the employing class [^mnp]. The result:

> "The Irish climbed on the Negroes. The Germans scrambled over the Negroes and emulated the Irish." [^mnp]

Empire did the rest. Colonial labor, the lowest paid in modern history, let capitalists buy off white workers with "high wages, visions of wealth" [^mnp].

---

So what of Marx and the American Negro? Du Bois kept the method but changed the map. Marxism, he wrote, "is a true diagnosis of the situation in Europe in the middle of the 19th Century," but "it must be modified in the United States of America and especially so far as the Negro group is concerned." [^mnp]

Black workers were exploited by white capitalists "and equally from the white proletariat." Their defense, he argued, was their own organization, with enough economic insight to stop a Black capitalist class from growing inside the race [^mnp].

Within a year he was urging voluntary Black 'self-dependence' in *The Crisis*, and he resigned from the NAACP over it in 1934 [^quits].

---

Du Bois closed on the Depression. It had shaken American industry, he wrote, yet white labor was still divided, and the call of the few communists for Black and white unity was ignored "because and solely because it seeks to break down barriers between black and white." [^mnp]

From this he drew his conclusion:

> "In the hearts of black laborers alone, therefore, lie those ideals of democracy in politics and industry which may in time make the workers of the world effective dictators of civilization." [^mnp]

Black workers, the most excluded, held the democratic hope for the whole working class.
