William Edward Burghardt Du Bois was born in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, on February 23, 1868, five years after the Emancipation Proclamation. He grew up as one of about 50 Black residents in a town of 5,000 [^obit].

He took degrees at Fisk University and then at Harvard, which awarded him a PhD in 1895. He taught at Wilberforce, the University of Pennsylvania and Atlanta University [^obit].

Du Bois lived to 95, from the end of slavery into the civil rights era. Over that long life he moved step by step from liberal reform toward socialism, and at last to communism. This lesson traces that road.

---

In 1909 Du Bois left Atlanta to help found the **NAACP** (National Association for the Advancement of Colored People), a civil rights organization, and to edit its magazine, *The Crisis* [^obit]. He edited 286 issues, from 1910 until 1934 [^crisis].

He was already reaching toward socialism. He joined the Socialist Party in 1911, but resigned to support the Democrat Woodrow Wilson for president [^cp61]. In *Socialism and the Negro Problem* (1913) he challenged the socialists on race [^snp]. In *The African Roots of War* (1915) he traced the First World War to the European scramble for Africa [^arw].

---

For the next twenty years, Du Bois later wrote, he attacked Democrats, Republicans and Socialists alike. In 1926 he began a "new effort," visiting Communist lands [^cp61].

The Great Depression sent him to Marx. *Marxism and the Negro Problem* (1933) asked how Marx's analysis fit Black workers in America [^mnp]. *Black Reconstruction* (1935) retold the years after the Civil War as a struggle of Black labor [^br].

In 1934 he resigned from *The Crisis* and the NAACP. He had been urging voluntary Black 'self-dependence' against white discrimination, and the board had barred salaried officers from criticizing its policy in the magazine [^quits].

---

After the Second World War, Du Bois worked for peace and African freedom. He headed the Council on African Affairs, and in 1949 chaired the Peace Information Center in New York, which sponsored a world peace petition that the Secretary of State called Communist-inspired [^obit].

In 1951 the government tried him for failing to register as a foreign agent. He was acquitted [^cp61].

In 1959 he met Nikita Khrushchev, the Soviet leader, in Moscow [^khr], received the Lenin Peace Prize, and was honored in Beijing on his 91st birthday [^obit]. In 1960 he spoke on *Socialism and the American Negro* in Madison, Wisconsin [^fw].

---

On October 1, 1961, at 93, Du Bois applied to join the **Communist Party of the United States** (CPUSA). The timing was pointed: the party faced fines of $10,000 a day for refusing to register under the 1950 Internal Security Act [^cp61].

He told the party's general secretary, Gus Hall, that he had been "long and slow" to decide, "but at last my mind is settled." His conclusion:

> "capitalism cannot reform itself; it is doomed to self-destruction." [^cp61]

Communism, he wrote, was "the effort to give all men what they need and to ask of each the best they can contribute" [^cp61].

---

By the time the party announced his membership, Du Bois was in Ghana, invited by President Kwame Nkrumah to direct an *Encyclopedia Africana*. He became a citizen of Ghana in 1963 and died there in August that year, aged 95 [^obit]. Ghana buried him with a state funeral [^ghana].

Du Bois came to communism late and by experience. He had tried the Socialist Party, the NAACP and decades of independent criticism before deciding, in his own words, that his mind was settled [^cp61]. The lessons that follow read him at each stage of that road.
