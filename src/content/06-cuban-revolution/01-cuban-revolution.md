In the 1950s, Cuba's land was held by **large landholders** and by the sugar and cattle magnates, with US companies behind them [^exc]. General Fulgencio Batista ruled as dictator after a coup in March 1952 [^hwam].

On July 26, 1953, a young lawyer named **Fidel Castro** led an armed attack on the Moncada army barracks in Santiago de Cuba [^hwam]. Before leaving, he told his comrades:

> "This movement will triumph anyway." [^moncada]

The attack failed, and many prisoners were murdered. At his trial Castro defended himself in a speech later published as *History Will Absolve Me* [^hwam].

---

On December 2, 1956, a group of 82 men landed from the boat *Granma* in Oriente province, in eastern Cuba. Three days later the army surprised them and almost wiped them out [^rem]. Among them was the young doctor **Ernesto 'Che' Guevara**.

The few survivors regrouped as guerrillas in the **Sierra Maestra** mountains. They had expected a quick uprising, and Guevara later called that hope 'subjectivist' [^notes]. In the mountains they learned otherwise:

> "They understood also that the fight would have to be a long one and that it would need vast campesino participation." [^notes]

(A *campesino* is a peasant.)

---

Many of the Sierra peasants had fled the landholders and joined because they wanted land of their own [^exc]. The guerrillas became the **Rebel Army**, and Batista's far larger army lost the will to fight [^notes]. On January 1, 1959, Batista fled the country [^jan59].

That day in Santiago, Castro recalled why he had believed 82 men could win: "Because we have the people." [^jan59]

On May 17, 1959, the new government passed an **Agrarian Law** that broke up the great estates. Owners kept part of their land and were paid in bonds, and Castro estimated that "two hundred thousand families will receive these benefits." [^agr]

---

Reform brought Cuba into conflict with the United States. In April 1961 an invasion force of exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs, supported, Castro said, by the US Navy and Air Force. Cuba's army and militia destroyed it "in less than 72 hours" [^pigs].

On May Day, Castro named what the revolution had become:

> "This is a socialist regime, yes! Yes, this is a socialist regime." [^mayday]

That same day, 100,000 young volunteers paraded on their way to the countryside to wipe out illiteracy [^mayday]. In 1962 Cuba answered its expulsion from the Organization of American States with the *Second Declaration of Havana*, a call to revolution across Latin America [^decl].

---

Guevara became a minister and a leading theorist. He held that moral incentives, not money, must drive the building of communism [^tribute], the theme of his *Socialism and Man in Cuba* (1965). In 1965 he gave up his posts and his Cuban citizenship to fight abroad:

> "Other nations of the world summon my modest efforts of assistance." [^farewell]

He fought in Bolivia, and his *Message to the Tricontinental* (1967) called for armed struggle across three continents. In 1967 his enemies there shot him after he had been seriously wounded in combat [^tribute]. Castro told Cuba the revolution had lost a man who was "only 39 years old at the time of his death" [^tribute].

---

The revolution also made grave errors and used harsh measures. In January 1959, revolutionary courts tried and shot police and army men of Batista's regime accused of murder. Castro defended the trials and said the number executed "will not be more than 400" [^rule]; the historian Luiz Alberto Moniz Bandeira puts it at about 550 [^bandeira].

The historian Helen Yaffe puts the exodus after 1959 at 65–75% of managers, technicians and engineers [^yaffe].

Through the 1960s and 1970s, Cuba sent openly gay men to labor camps without charge or trial [^cnn]. About 30,000 people passed through the UMAP camps of 1965–1968 alone, by the historian Abel Sierra Madero's count [^sierra]. In 2010 Castro took responsibility:

> "They were moments of great injustice, great injustice!" [^cnn]

---

The revolution survived a US blockade that, by 2003, had lasted almost half a century [^may03]. In 1980 about 125,000 Cubans left for the United States from the port of Mariel [^mariel]. After the Soviet bloc fell, GDP dropped nearly 35% between 1989 and 1993 and calorie intake by more than 30%, by the count of Cuba's former economy minister José Luis Rodríguez [^rodriguez]. In 2003 Castro pointed to gains in health:

> "Infant mortality has been reduced from 60 per 1000 live births to a rate that fluctuates between 6 and 6.5" [^may03]

Cuba showed that a small, poor country could make a socialist revolution ninety miles from the United States [^mayday]. The lessons that follow read the central works of Castro and Guevara.
