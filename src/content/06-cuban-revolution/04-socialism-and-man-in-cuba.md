In early 1965, Che Guevara, one of the leaders of the Cuban Revolution, was touring Africa. He wrote a long letter to Carlos Quijano, editor of *Marcha*, a weekly in Montevideo, Uruguay. It appeared on March 12, 1965 [^man].

Guevara answered a common charge of capitalism's spokespeople: that building socialism means

> "the abolition of the individual for the sake of the state." [^man]

He would not reply with theory alone. He would first set out the facts as they existed in Cuba [^man].

---

In the guerrilla war, Guevara wrote, the individual was decisive: every commander had earned rank through outstanding deeds. After 1959 a new actor appeared: **the mass**. The mass is not a flock of sheep. It follows Fidel Castro because he has interpreted its wishes and fought to keep his promises [^man].

At great rallies, Guevara saw "something like the dialogue of two tuning forks whose vibrations interact, producing new sounds" [^man].

The state also makes mistakes, and then enthusiasm drops and work stalls. Guevara pointed to the sectarian policy imposed on the party in 1962. A firmer connection with the mass, he admitted, was still needed [^man].

---

Under capitalism, people are ruled by laws they cannot see:

> "The alienated human specimen is tied to society as a whole by an invisible umbilical cord: the law of value." [^man]

The **law of value** is the market's rule that goods exchange according to the labor time needed to make them. **Alienation** is people's estrangement from their own work and lives. The market shapes every life, yet the propaganda shows only the chance of individual success, like Rockefeller's. Guevara saw it otherwise: "it is a contest among wolves. One can win only at the cost of the failure of others." [^man]

---

Countries like Cuba, Guevara argued, broke from capitalism while still poor. The temptation is to speed growth with capitalism's own tools: profit and individual material interest. He warned:

> "The pipe dream that socialism can be achieved with the help of the dull instruments left to us by capitalism (the commodity as the economic cell, profitability, individual material interest as a lever, etc.) can lead into a blind alley." [^man]

"To build communism it is necessary, simultaneous with the new material foundations, to build the new man and woman." [^man] That calls for **moral incentives** (recognition, duty, solidarity) and education. "Society as a whole must be converted into a gigantic school." [^man]

---

Work itself must change. When the means of production belong to society, work can become a social duty and an expression of oneself, not labor power sold to survive. Cuba's **voluntary work** was a step. Guevara was frank: "there are still coercive aspects to work, even when it is voluntary." [^man]

Leading the way is the **vanguard**: the party, made of the best workers, a minority that leads by example. It should become a mass party only when the masses reach its level [^man].

Guevara admitted that the revolution's institutions were unfinished: "This institutionalization of the revolution has not yet been achieved." [^man]

---

The vanguard revolutionary carries a heavy load. Guevara wrote:

> "At the risk of seeming ridiculous, let me say that the true revolutionary is guided by great feelings of love." [^man]

That love is for the people as a whole, and must become deeds that set an example. Its duty is international: if zeal fades once local tasks are done, the revolution sinks into "a comfortable drowsiness" [^man].

He closed with a paradox: "We socialists are freer because we are more fulfilled; we are more fulfilled because we are freer." [^man]

---

**The debate:** From 1963 to 1965, Cuban Marxists argued over how to run the economy in the **Great Debate**. One side backed the Soviet system of self-financing enterprises. In the historian Helen Yaffe's words, "The Soviet solution was to rely on the operation of the law of value to hasten the development of the productive forces" [^yaffe]. Guevara instead built a centrally planned **Budgetary Finance System**. By 1966 he concluded that the USSR "is returning to capitalism." [^yaffe]

Soviet-style methods later spread in Cuba. In 1987, in the middle of a **rectification** campaign against them, Castro said that "Che was radically opposed to using and developing capitalist economic laws and categories in building socialism" [^rect].
