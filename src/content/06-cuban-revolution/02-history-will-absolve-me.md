In March 1952, General Fulgencio Batista seized power in Cuba in a coup [^hwam]. At dawn on July 26, 1953, Fidel Castro, a young lawyer, led 165 men in an attack on the Moncada army barracks in Santiago de Cuba [^hwam]. The attack was defeated.

The regime kept him in solitary confinement and his lawyer could not see him in private, so Castro used his own right as a lawyer to defend himself:

> "In this case, counsel and defendant are one and the same." [^hwam]

He turned his defense into an indictment of the dictatorship, and a program for the revolution to come [^hwam].

---

Castro first told the court what the regime had hidden. After the attack, Batista's army hunted down the rebels and killed its prisoners:

> "Before Batista spoke, more than 25 prisoners had been murdered. After Batista spoke fifty more were massacred." [^hwam]

Batista called the rebels hirelings paid by a former president. Castro answered that "with less than 20,000 pesos, we armed 165 men" [^hwam], raised by young people who sold their jobs, tools and savings. The movement had no link with the old parties. It was "a new Cuban generation with its own ideas, rising up against tyranny" [^hwam].

---

Why had the rebels expected the people to back them? Castro defined whom he meant by **the people**:

> "When we speak of struggle and we mention the people we mean the vast unredeemed masses, those to whom everyone makes promises and who are deceived by all" [^hwam]

He counted them: six hundred thousand Cubans without work, five hundred thousand farm laborers, four hundred thousand industrial workers, one hundred thousand small farmers, and the teachers, small traders and young professionals with no future [^hwam]. The comfortable classes, he said, welcome any dictatorship.

---

Had Moncada fallen, five **revolutionary laws** would have been broadcast at once [^hwam]. The first restored the Constitution of 1940. The second gave ownership of the land to tenants, sharecroppers and squatters on small plots, with compensation to the former owners.

The third gave workers the right "to share 30% of the profits" of the large enterprises, including the sugar mills. The fourth gave sugar planters 55% of the crop. The fifth seized the "ill-gotten gains" of corrupt officials of past regimes [^hwam].

These were reforms within the existing Constitution, not yet a socialist program. But they aimed straight at the landholders and the monopolies.

---

Castro then named six problems: land, industrialization, housing, unemployment, education and health [^hwam]. More than half of the best farmland was in foreign hands, and Cuba stayed a producer of raw materials:

> "We export sugar to import candy, we export hides to import shoes, we export iron to import plows" [^hwam]

The cure would not come from a dozen big businessmen. In Castro's words, "social problems are not solved by spontaneous generation" [^hwam]. A revolutionary government would expropriate excess land, halve rents, industrialize, and pay teachers properly.

---

Finally, Castro defended the **right of rebellion**. He traced it through Locke, Rousseau, the American Declaration of Independence and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man, and back to Cuba's own wars of independence [^hwam]. A government that seized power by force had no claim on obedience.

He did not ask the court to free him while his comrades sat in prison. He closed:

> "Condemn me. It does not matter. History will absolve me." [^hwam]

---

Less than six years later, Castro headed a revolutionary government. Its **Agrarian Law** of May 1959 put the land program into effect. Castro promised that "two million Cubans will have their income increased" [^agr].

In 1961, proclaiming the revolution socialist, Castro reached back to the trial and read a line from it: "No social problem is resolved spontaneously." [^mayday] The program for democratic reform had grown into a socialist revolution, because the landholders and monopolies would accept no reform at all.
