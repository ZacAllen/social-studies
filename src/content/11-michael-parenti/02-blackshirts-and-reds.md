In New York's Little Italy, Michael Parenti (1933–2026), an American Marxist political scientist [^mia], once found a shop selling Mussolini T-shirts. The clerk told him "maybe we need someone like Mussolini in this country" [^ch1]. Fascism, Parenti concluded, is more than a historical curiosity.

He opened *Blackshirts and Reds* (1997) with the chapter *Rational Fascism* [^book] [^ch1]. Most books on fascism, he complained, say little about its class policies: how the regimes treated taxes, business, labor and social services. He asked the missing question:

> "For whose benefit and at whose expense?" [^ch1]

---

Benito Mussolini, a blacksmith's son born in 1883, began as a socialist and edited the Socialist Party's newspaper. When the Italian upper class offered him money and power, he switched sides. He went from organizing strikes to breaking them, leading the **Blackshirts**, gangs of ex-officers and toughs who attacked unionists, socialists and farm cooperatives [^ch1].

After World War I, Italy's economy was in recession. To restore profits, owners wanted lower wages, state subsidies and cuts to social services. But by 1921 many workers and peasants were unionized and had won real gains [^ch1]. So:

> "The solution was to smash their unions, political organizations, and civil liberties." [^ch1]

---

In 1922, Parenti writes, leaders of industry, banking and agribusiness met Mussolini to plan the **March on Rome**, contributing 20 million lire. Backed by top army officers and police chiefs, the fascist 'revolution' was really a coup [^ch1].

Within two years, by his account, Mussolini had shut down opposition newspapers and crushed the parties that together had won some 80 percent of the vote. Labor and peasant leaders were beaten, exiled or murdered [^ch1].

The Italian Communist Party "endured the severest repression of all" [^ch1]. It kept up an underground resistance that grew into armed struggle against the Blackshirts and the German occupiers [^ch1].

---

Germany followed a similar pattern. Business funded the **SA** (the Nazi Stormtroopers) to terrorize workers. By 1930, most tycoons had decided the **Weimar Republic** (Germany's democracy after 1918) was too accommodating to workers, and raised their funding of Hitler [^ch1].

In July 1932 the Nazis won 37.3 percent of the vote, their best result in a democratic national election. "They never had a majority of the people on their side" [^ch1]. Their surest base was the affluent.

> "But the great majority of the organized working class supported the Communists or Social Democrats to the very end." [^ch1]

In January 1933, President Hindenburg made Hitler chancellor. The Nazis crushed organized labor, and hundreds of thousands of opponents were imprisoned, tortured or murdered [^ch1].

---

Why call fascism 'rational'? One section of the chapter is titled 'The Rational Use of Irrational Ideology' [^book]. Italy and Germany differed in history and language, yet reached the same repressive solutions, because their economic power and class conflict were alike [^ch1]. Fascism arose there, and in Spain and Hungary too, "to save big capital from the impositions of democracy" [^ch1]. He noted real differences too, above all the horrific role of anti-Semitism in Nazi Germany [^ch1].

The Communist International (the world federation of communist parties) agreed. Reporting to its Seventh World Congress in 1935, Georgi Dimitrov said that fascism in power was:

> "the open terrorist dictatorship of the most reactionary, most chauvinistic and most imperialist elements of finance capital." [^dimitrov]

---

**The debate:** Why could Germany's huge workers' movement not stop Hitler? Parenti blamed the **Social Democrats** (the reformist socialist party): their leaders refused the Communists' proposal of a last-minute coalition against Nazism [^ch1].

Leon Trotsky, the exiled Bolshevik, blamed the Communist leadership too. In 1931 he charged that it thought fascism could not be beaten "without first defeating the Social Democracy" [^trotsky]. He urged joint action with no common program: "March separately, but strike together!" [^trotsky]

In 1935, Dimitrov's report held the Social Democrats responsible for splitting the working class, but also admitted Communist mistakes, starting with:

> "an impermissible underestimation of the fascist danger" [^dimitrov]

His report called for a united front of the working class against fascism [^dimitrov].
