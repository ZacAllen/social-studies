Most Americans believe they have a free press, and many hear that it leans liberal. Michael Parenti (1933–2026), an American Marxist political scientist [^mia], argued otherwise. His book *Inventing Reality* (second edition 1993) presents the news media as an institution of the capitalist system, serving the rich while appearing to serve the many [^bio] [^book].

In a 2001 essay, *Monopoly Media Manipulation*, he summed up its methods. Reporters do make honest mistakes, but mistakes cannot explain the pattern [^mmm]:

> "media bias usually does not occur in random fashion; rather it moves in more or less consistent directions" [^mmm]

It favors management over labor, officialdom over protesters, and U.S. dominance over revolutionary change [^mmm].

---

The first method is **suppression by omission**. "More insidious than the sensationalistic hype is the artful avoidance" [^mmm]. In 1965 the Indonesian army, backed by the United States, overthrew President Sukarno and killed half a million people, by some estimates a million. *Time* gave it passing mention three months later [^mmm].

When omission fails, the press attacks. In 1996 the *San Jose Mercury News* reported on CIA-linked crack shipments into Los Angeles. Once the story spread, the major media launched "an all-out assault" calling it "bad journalism" and painting the reporter Gary Webb as irresponsible [^mmm].

---

The next tool is **labeling**: fixing a word on a subject so readers judge it before seeing the facts. 'Stability' and 'a strong defense' sound good. 'Leftist guerrillas' and 'conspiracy theories' sound bad [^mmm].

A favorite is 'reform', whose meaning is turned inside out. It names any policy that undoes gains won by decades of popular struggle, as in 'welfare reform' or the 'reforms' that impoverished Eastern Europe [^mmm]. Anyone who resists is a 'hardliner'.

Parenti noted what goes missing:

> "Some labels we will never be exposed to are 'class power,' 'class struggle,' and 'U.S. imperialism.'" [^mmm]

---

In **face-value transmission**, the press passes on official claims without question [^mmm]. Reporters say they must keep their own views out. Parenti answered that they already put them in without noticing, since their views usually match their bosses'. "This uniformity of bias is perceived as 'objectivity'" [^mmm].

**False balancing** gives the look of fairness. A 1997 BBC report spoke of "a history of violence between Indonesian forces and Timorese guerrillas" [^mmm]. It never said the guerrillas were fighting an invasion force that had slaughtered some 200,000 Timorese [^mmm].

> "While giving the appearance of being objective and neutral, one actually neutralizes the subject matter and thereby drastically warps it." [^mmm]

---

Parenti's central point is about **framing**: the packaging, placement, tone, headlines and pictures that shape a story [^mmm].

> "The most effective propaganda relies on framing rather than on falsehood." [^mmm]

Framing also hides causes. In the news, "fighting broke out" and recessions just happen, like weather [^mmm]. Explain bad labor conditions by corporate class power, Parenti wrote, and editors reject it as "Marxist" [^mmm]. His reply: "which indeed it is, as is much of reality itself" [^mmm].

---

So the press does not fail at its job. Parenti called its daily performance "not a failure but a skillfully evasive success" [^mmm].

> "Media people have a trained incapacity for the whole truth." [^mmm]

That changes what a reader does. Instead of complaining that reporters 'got it wrong', Parenti urged a move "from a liberal complaint about the press's sloppy performance to a radical analysis of how the media maintain the dominant paradigm" [^mmm]. Ask who owns the outlet, which labels it uses, what it leaves out, and who benefits from the frame.
