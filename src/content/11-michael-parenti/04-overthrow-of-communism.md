Between 1989 and 1991, the communist governments of Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union fell. Many Western left intellectuals cheered: the Left, they said, would be freed from the stigma of 'Stalinism' [^rollback].

Michael Parenti (1933–2026), an American Marxist political scientist [^mia], did not join the chorus. He took up the question in *Blackshirts and Reds* (1997), which traces the forces inside and outside that brought communism down, and the damage that followed [^book].

> "We feared that it was a historic defeat for the people of the world." [^rollback]

What had been lost? To answer, he first looked at what the communist countries actually were.

---

Whether to call them 'socialist', Parenti wrote, is a matter of definition. But "they constituted something different from what existed in the profit-driven capitalist world" [^ch3]. He named four differences.

There was far less inequality: the Soviet income spread was about five to one, against about 10,000 to one in the United States. Public ownership replaced private ownership, so no one could grow rich by hiring others. Education, jobs, housing and medical care were guaranteed. And these states did not invest abroad to seize weaker nations' land, labor and resources [^ch3].

Life there "left a lot to be desired" and services were rarely the best, he admitted, but the security was real [^ch3].

---

The Soviet state was born under attack. In 1918–1920, fourteen capitalist nations, including the United States, invaded Soviet Russia to overthrow the Bolsheviks. Parenti called the result **siege socialism**: centralized power and party unity, built to survive [^ch3].

By the late 1920s, the Soviets chose forced industrialization, building a huge industrial base east of the Ural Mountains. When the Nazis invaded in 1941, that base produced the weapons that turned the tide. The cost, by Parenti's count, included 22 million Soviet dead [^ch3].

The same siege left lasting damage:

> "The very siege socialism that allowed the USSR to survive made it difficult for it to thrive." [^ch4]

---

Siege did not excuse everything. Parenti wrote that it did not make inevitable:

> "the heartless execution of hundreds of Old Bolshevik leaders … and the mass deportations of 'suspect' nationalities." [^ch3]

(The **Old Bolsheviks** were the party's veterans of the revolution.) The party itself later admitted the scale. In 1956, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev told the party's 20th Congress that 98 of the 139 Central Committee members elected at the 17th Congress had been arrested and shot, mostly in 1937–1938 [^khrushchev].

The archives give an official figure of 681,692 executions in 1937–38, and leading Russian specialists thought about 1 million possible. The historian R. W. Davies called even the official figure "an appalling indictment of the Soviet regime" [^davies].

---

Parenti also faced the system's internal problems. Central planning had built steel mills and tanks, but it "eventually hindered technological development and growth" and could not supply enough consumer goods [^ch4]. Managers had no reason to innovate, and prices bore little relation to cost [^ch4].

People took free schooling and health care for granted while longing for Western consumer goods. In 1989 the East German ambassador in Washington told Parenti ruefully:

> "We thought building a good society would make good people. That's not always true." [^ch4]

When perestroika (the restructuring led by the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev) opened the economy, Parenti wrote, "socialism was used to subsidize and build an unforgiving capitalism" [^ch4].

---

The aftermath confirmed Parenti's fears. In the former socialist countries came mass privatization, deindustrialization, and sharp rises in poverty, unemployment, crime and disease [^rollback].

The West changed too. During the Cold War, competition with communism had pushed Western rulers into concessions. "One of the things that helped workers win concessions was 'the threat of communism'" [^rollback]. With that rival gone, the corporate class began rolling back wages, services and protections [^rollback].

> "'Capitalism with a human face' has become capitalism in your face." [^rollback]

Without Soviet aid, liberation movements also lost a key ally. Nelson Mandela had spoken of the "essential aid" the African National Congress received [^rollback].

---

**The debate:** Some Western leftists answered that socialism never existed in these countries. They were bureaucratic "state capitalism", and real socialism would be run directly by the workers [^ch3]. The American writer Noam Chomsky even called 1917 the "Bolshevik counterrevolution" [^ch3].

Parenti called this 'pure socialism' view ahistorical: it compares an ideal against an imperfect reality. Its supporters never explain how a revolution would beat invasion and sabotage without a strong state [^ch3].

> "No surprise then that the pure socialists support every revolution except the ones that succeed." [^ch3]

He was blunt about the cost of their anticommunism. The real enemy, he wrote in 2002, "is not Stalin (who incidentally is dead)" but the Western leaders running "the cruelest scam in history" [^rollback].
