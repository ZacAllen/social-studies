Michael Parenti (1933–2026) was an American Marxist political scientist and historian [^mia]. Born into an Italian-American working-class family in New York City, he earned a Ph.D. in political science at Yale [^bio]. The Vietnam War radicalized him. Looking back, he wrote:

> "At that point I started moving from a liberal complaint about how bad things are to a radical analysis about why they are the way they are." [^boggs]

His activism cost him a regular university post, so he became a public intellectual and wrote twenty-four books [^boggs] [^bio]. Later lessons take up his books on fascism (*Blackshirts and Reds*, 1997), on the news media (*Inventing Reality*), and on the fall of communism [^bio].

---

This lesson follows *Imperialism 101*, the opening chapter of *Against Empire* (1995) [^book] [^imp101]. Parenti called imperialism the most powerful force in world history over the last four or five centuries, yet one that is seldom given serious attention. When it is discussed, empires become "commonwealths" and colonies become "territories" [^imp101]. He started with a plain definition:

> "By 'imperialism' I mean the process whereby the dominant politico-economic interests of one nation expropriate for their own enrichment the land, labor, raw materials, and markets of another people." [^imp101]

The ancient Persian, Roman and Mongol empires were after plunder and tribute. Capitalist imperialism goes further: it invests in other countries and folds them into "an international system of capital accumulation" [^imp101].

---

Why does capitalism reach abroad? "A central imperative of capitalism is expansion" [^imp101]. Investors put money in only to get more back, so the hunt for new resources, markets and cheap labor never stops.

Parenti gave figures for the early 1990s. North American and European corporations controlled more than three-fourths of the known mineral resources of the **Third World** (the poor countries of Asia, Africa and Latin America). U.S. corporate profit rates there were 50 percent higher than in developed countries [^imp101].

Some writers argued capitalism could survive without its Third World investments. Parenti answered that survival is not all capitalists want:

> "Imperial relations may not be the only way to pursue profits, but they are the most lucrative way." [^imp101]

---

We are taught that poor countries were always poor, because of bad soil, lazy people or too many children. Parenti turned this around: Europeans went to Asia, Africa and Latin America because those lands held great treasures. "The Third World is rich. Only its people are poor" [^imp101].

India is his example. In 1810 India exported more textiles to England than England exported to India. By 1830 British tariffs and dumping, backed by gunboats, had reversed the flow. From 1850 to 1900, India's per capita income fell by almost two-thirds [^imp101].

> "British imperialism did two things: first, it ended India's development, then it forcibly underdeveloped that country." [^imp101]

So most of the Third World is "not 'underdeveloped' but overexploited" [^imp101].

---

The leading Western theory, called **modernization** or development theory, held that rich nations would uplift poor ones with investment and know-how. Parenti called it an updated "the White man's burden" [^imp101]. In reality, "Investors go into a country not to uplift it but to enrich themselves" [^imp101].

What results is **maldevelopment**: luxury hotels instead of housing for the poor, cash crops for export instead of food for local markets, and highways from the mines to the ports instead of roads to villages [^imp101]. The poor need their resources back, not lectures:

> "People in these countries do not need to be taught how to farm. They need the land and the implements to farm." [^imp101]

---

After World War II, almost all colonies won formal independence. Parenti called what followed **neoimperialism**: weak countries get the trappings of sovereignty while Western finance capital keeps their most profitable resources. The United States pioneered it in Cuba, which it took from Spain in 1898, then made formally independent while keeping its wealth [^imp101]. "Under neoimperialism, the flag stays home, while the dollar goes everywhere" [^imp101].

The system relies on a **comprador class**, "one that cooperates in turning its own country into a client state for foreign interests" [^imp101]. Still, formal independence breeds hopes of real independence, and sometimes a patriot rather than a collaborator takes power. So the change was "a net gain for popular forces in the world" [^imp101].

---

By 2000, even conservative pundits were calling the United States an empire. In a 2010 essay, Parenti warned that the word had been emptied of its content. People talked of power and prestige, but rarely of the profits that power serves [^empires]. His correction:

> "Imperialism is what empires do." [^empires]

That is why empires kill, he argued: "The purpose of all this killing is to prevent alternative, independent, self-defining nations from emerging" [^empires]. To understand a war, ask who profits and which independent path it closes off. That question runs through all of Parenti's work.
