On September 7, 1937, Mao wrote a short warning to the Communist Party and the revolutionary organizations around it [^cl]. It was about a habit that could rot them from within. He called it **liberalism**.

He did not mean a political program. He meant an attitude: avoiding argument to keep the peace. His opening:

> "We stand for active ideological struggle because it is the weapon for ensuring unity within the Party and the revolutionary organizations in the interest of our fight." [^cl]

Liberalism, by contrast, "rejects ideological struggle and stands for unprincipled peace" [^cl].

---

Mao listed eleven types. The first is to let a comrade's clear mistake slide because he is a friend, a schoolmate or a fellow townsman, "so as to keep on good terms" [^cl]. Both the organization and the person are harmed.

The second is the most familiar:

> "To say nothing to people to their faces but to gossip behind their backs, or to say nothing at a meeting but to gossip afterwards." [^cl]

The third is to keep quiet about what is wrong when it doesn't touch you personally, "to be worldly wise and play safe and seek only to avoid blame" [^cl].

---

The middle types concern discipline and the masses. The fourth is to demand special consideration from the organization while rejecting its discipline. The fifth is to replace argument with personal attacks, quarrels and revenge.

The sixth is to hear incorrect views and let them pass. The seventh is to live among the masses without doing political work or caring for their well-being, "forgetting that one is a Communist" [^cl]. The eighth is to see someone harming the masses and not feel indignant.

We could name more, Mao wrote, but these are the principal types: "They are all manifestations of liberalism." [^cl]

---

The last three types concern work and self-respect. The ninth is to work half-heartedly, without plan or direction, as in the saying "So long as one remains a monk, one goes on tolling the bell" [^cl].

The tenth is to pride oneself on being a veteran, to disdain small assignments while being unequal to large ones, and to be "slipshod in work and slack in study" [^cl].

The eleventh is to see one's own mistakes and make no attempt to correct them: "taking a liberal attitude towards oneself" [^cl].

---

Where does liberalism come from? Mao's answer was class: it "stems from petty-bourgeois selfishness" [^cl], putting personal interests first and the revolution second.

He described the result sharply. Such people approve of Marxism but will not practice it in full:

> "they talk Marxism but practice liberalism; they apply Marxism to others but liberalism to themselves." [^cl]

Liberalism, he concluded, eats away unity, breeds apathy and dissension, and cuts the party off from the masses.

---

The remedy is a Communist who looks upon "the interests of the revolution as his very life" [^cl] and wages a tireless struggle against wrong ideas, in order to strengthen the party's unity.

In the **rectification movement** of 1942, Mao spelled out how such struggle should be waged. Errors must be exposed without sparing anyone's feelings, but the aim is to save the comrade:

> "In treating an ideological or a political malady, one must never be rough and rash but must adopt the approach of 'curing the sickness to save the patient', which is the only correct and effective method." [^rectify]

Principled struggle, in other words, serves unity rather than destroying it.
