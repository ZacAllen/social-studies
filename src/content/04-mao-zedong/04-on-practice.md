In July 1937, Mao gave a lecture at the Anti-Japanese Military and Political College in Yan'an (spelled Yenan in older translations) [^op].

His target was a problem inside the party. **Dogmatists** quoted "words and phrases from Marxist works, torn out of context" [^op], while **empiricists** trusted only their own fragmentary experience. The party's editors later wrote that the dogmatists' errors "caused enormous losses to the Chinese revolution during 1931-34" [^op].

Mao's answer was a Marxist **theory of knowledge**: an account of where knowledge comes from and how we know it is true. Its subtitle: *On the Relation Between Knowledge and Practice, Between Knowing and Doing*.

---

Mao starts with production. People come to know nature, and their relations with one another, through the work of meeting their material needs. That is "the primary source from which human knowledge develops" [^op].

**Practice** also takes other forms: class struggle, political life, science and art. In class society, it shapes how everyone thinks:

> "In class society everyone lives as a member of a particular class, and every kind of thinking, without exception, is stamped with the brand of a class." [^op]

So the test of an idea is not how it feels. "Only social practice can be the criterion of truth." [^op]

---

How does knowledge grow out of practice? Mao gave an example: visitors touring Yan'an. In the first days, they see streets and houses, attend meetings and banquets, and hear all kinds of talk. This is **perceptual knowledge**: impressions of the separate aspects of things.

As impressions repeat, "a sudden change (leap) takes place in the brain" [^op], and concepts form. This is **rational knowledge**, which grasps "the essence, the totality and the internal relations of things" [^op]. The visitors can now judge the party's policy as a whole.

Each stage needs the other. Reason without experience is "a tree without roots" [^op]. Experience without theory stays shallow.

---

Knowledge, then, begins in experience of changing the world:

> "If you want to know the taste of a pear, you must change the pear by eating it yourself." [^op]

The same goes for atoms and for revolution: "If you want to know the theory and methods of revolution, you must take part in revolution." [^op]

Of course, no one can experience everything. Most knowledge comes indirectly, from other times and places. But what is indirect for me was direct for someone else. Mao mocked the 'know-all' who picks up "a smattering of hearsay knowledge" [^op] and proclaims himself an authority.

---

Then comes the more important leap: from theory back to practice. Theory matters only because it can guide action. Applied to the world, it is tested, corrected and developed, and failure itself teaches: "failure is the mother of success" [^op].

The process never ends, because the world keeps changing. Mao summed it up:

> "Practice, knowledge, again practice, and again knowledge." [^op]

Each cycle, he wrote, raises practice and knowledge to a higher level.

---

The theory cut both ways. Mao opposed the **die-hards**, whose thinking lags behind a changing situation, and also 'Left' phrase-mongers who "strain to realize in the present an ideal which can only be realized in the future" [^op]. Both, he wrote, separate knowledge from practice.

The idea outlived him. In December 1978, the party's Central Committee "highly evaluated the forum on the criterion of truth" [^res], taking up the principle of *On Practice*. Party members, it said, were freeing themselves from "the personality cult and the dogmatism that prevailed in the past" [^res]. No idea, including a leader's, is true until practice proves it.
