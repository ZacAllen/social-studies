In May 1930, Mao was a leader of the Fourth Army of the Red Army, which had come down from the Jinggang Mountains to build base areas in southern Jiangxi and western Fujian [^obw]. Its officers kept issuing orders without knowing local conditions, and quoting the party's resolutions as if they settled everything.

Mao wrote a short, sharp pamphlet against this habit. It opens:

> "Unless you have investigated a problem, you will be deprived of the right to speak on it." [^obw]

Is that too harsh? "Not in the least," he answered [^obw]. Without investigation, talk is nonsense, and nonsense solves no problems.

---

What if a problem seems too hard? Then go and investigate its present facts and its past history. "Conclusions invariably come after investigation, and not before." [^obw]

Mao mocked leaders who simply sighed and asked for a transfer when they hit a hard problem. Walk around every section in your charge, he told them, or call a meeting of people who know the situation. An empty head will come back full.

> "Investigation may be likened to the long months of pregnancy, and solving a problem to the day of birth." [^obw]

---

Then the target of the title. Some comrades always said, "Show me where it's written in the book" [^obw]. Mao called this **book worship**: treating texts, or orders from above, as right simply because they are written down.

Marxism is not true because Marx was a prophet, he argued:

> "When we say Marxism is correct, it is certainly not because Marx was a 'prophet' but because his theory has been proved correct in our practice and in our struggle." [^obw]

So study Marxist books, but integrate them with China's actual conditions. "We need books, but we must overcome book worship" [^obw].

---

What should investigation look for? Not a pile of curiosities "as trivial as a grocer's accounts" [^obw], but the relations between classes.

> "Our chief method of investigation must be to dissect the different social classes" [^obw]

Mao listed thirteen classes and strata, from the industrial proletariat and the farm laborers to the landlords and the commercial bourgeoisie. In the villages, it was not enough to count owners and tenants. One had to know who were rich, middle and poor peasants. Only then could the party decide which classes were its main force, which were allies, and which had to be overthrown.

---

Correct tactics, Mao insisted, cannot be drawn up in advance:

> "A Communist Party's correct and unswerving tactics of struggle can in no circumstance be created by a few people sitting in an office; they emerge in the course of mass struggle, that is, through actual experience." [^obw]

Some comrades treated the resolutions of the party's Sixth Congress (1928) as a guarantee of lasting victory [^obw]. Mao called this a conservative line. Instead, leaders at every level, "from the chairman of the township government to the chairman of the central government" [^obw], had to investigate personally, holding fact-finding meetings of at least three well-informed people.

---

Eleven years later, Mao gave this method a name: to **seek truth from facts**. He explained each word:

> "'Facts' are all the things that exist objectively, 'truth' means their internal relations, that is, the laws governing them, and 'to seek' means to study." [^reform]

Theory, he wrote, is the "arrow" and the Chinese revolution the "target" [^reform]. Book worship shoots at random. Seeking truth from facts aims. That idea runs through the rest of Mao's philosophy, and next through *On Practice*.
