By 1957, China's farms and workshops had been organized into cooperatives, and private firms turned into joint state-private enterprises [^ch]. But all was not calm. In socialist Hungary, an uprising (Mao called it 'the Hungarian incident') had broken out, and in 1956 small numbers of Chinese workers and students had gone on strike [^ch].

On February 27, 1957, Mao spoke to the Supreme State Conference, a gathering of the country's leaders [^ch]. His question: what conflicts remain in a socialist society, and how should they be handled? He opened confidently:

> "Never before has our country been as united as it is today." [^ch]

He revised the speech before it was published that June [^ch].

---

Mao built on his 1937 distinction between kinds of contradiction. There are **contradictions between ourselves and the enemy**, which are antagonistic, and **contradictions among the people**, which usually are not [^ch].

Who are 'the people'? In the war against Japan, everyone who resisted Japan. In 1957, every class and group that supported building socialism.

The two types need different methods:

> "the former entail drawing a clear distinction between ourselves and the enemy, and the latter entail drawing a clear distinction between right and wrong." [^ch]

---

Contradictions among the people include those between workers and peasants, and between workers and the **national bourgeoisie**: Chinese capitalists who accepted socialist transformation. That one was antagonistic by nature, Mao said, but could be resolved peacefully if handled properly [^ch].

He went further. Even a government that serves the people has conflicts with them:

> "there are still certain contradictions between this government and the people." [^ch]

Among them were the contradictions arising from "the bureaucratic style of work of some of the state personnel" [^ch]. The strikes of 1956, he said, were caused above all by bureaucracy on the part of the leadership.

---

Contradictions among the people must not be settled by force. "We cannot abolish religion by administrative order or force people not to believe in it." [^ch]

Mao's method came from the party's 1942 rectification movement:

> "This democratic method of resolving contradictions among the people was epitomized in 1942 in the formula 'unity -- criticism -- unity'." [^ch]

Start from the desire for unity, resolve differences through criticism, and arrive at a new unity. It replaced the old method of "ruthless struggle and merciless blows" [^ch].

---

In arts and science, the policy was **"Let a hundred flowers blossom, let a hundred schools of thought contend"** [^ch]. Even Marxism could be criticized:

> "Marxism is scientific truth and fears no criticism." [^ch]

Fighting wrong ideas, Mao said, is like vaccination. Plants raised in hothouses are unlikely to be hardy [^ch].

He also set six political criteria to tell "fragrant flowers" from "poisonous weeds". The most important were the socialist path and the leadership of the party [^ch].

---

The practice fell short of the speech. In 1957, critics invited to speak out in a party rectification campaign attacked the party's leadership. The party struck back in the **Anti-Rightist campaign**, and its own 1981 Resolution judged that "the scope of this struggle was made far too broad" [^res].

In 1958 came the **Great Leap Forward**, a mass campaign for rapid growth, together with rural **people's communes**. The Resolution blamed 'Left' errors and leaders who "overestimated the role of man's subjective will and efforts" [^res]. The result was famine in 1959–61. Demographers first estimated the toll "between 16.5 and 23 million" [^smil]; later studies found 23 to 30 million excess deaths [^smil].

---

**The debate:** From 1962, Mao argued that the bourgeoisie would persist throughout socialism and find its source inside the party itself [^res]. On that basis he launched the **Cultural Revolution** (1966–76). In rural China alone it left an estimated 750,000 to 1.5 million dead, and 36 million politically persecuted [^walder].

In 1981 the party gave its verdict. Mao bore chief responsibility, and the Cultural Revolution "did not in fact constitute a revolution or social progress in any sense" [^res]. It had confused the people with the enemy: the very error this speech warned against. Yet it judged his record as a whole:

> "His merits are primary and his errors secondary." [^res]
