In early 1927, the Communists and the Kuomintang (the nationalist party) were allies in a war against China's warlords. As their armies marched north, peasants in Hunan province rose against their landlords. In the cities, many people, Communists included, were alarmed.

Mao Zedong went to see for himself. For thirty-two days, from January 4 to February 5, he held fact-finding meetings in five counties with peasants and organizers [^rep]. He came back certain:

> "several hundred million peasants will rise like a mighty storm, like a hurricane, a force so swift and violent that no power, however great, will be able to hold it back" [^rep]

---

The peasants organized through **peasant associations**. Membership in Hunan jumped from 300,000–400,000 to two million in a few months. Since one name usually stood for a whole family, that meant about ten million people: almost half the province's peasants [^rep].

Every party, Mao warned, would be tested by this upsurge. He put the choice plainly:

> "To march at their head and lead them? To trail behind them, gesticulating and criticizing? Or to stand in their way and oppose them?" [^rep]

---

The main targets were the **local tyrants and evil gentry** (the landlords and rural notables who ran the villages, taxed them and judged them). In the cities, their friends had one phrase for the uprising: "It's terrible!" [^rep]

Mao turned the phrase around. The peasants were overthrowing a feudal class that had ruled for thousands of years, he argued. It had achieved in a few months what Sun Yat-sen, the nationalist leader, had failed to do in forty years.

> "What the peasants are doing is absolutely right, what they are doing is fine!" [^rep]

To call it 'terrible', he wrote, was a landlord theory.

---

Middle-of-the-roaders said the peasants were "going too far" [^rep]. Mao did not hide what happened: landlords were fined, paraded in tall paper hats, jailed, and the worst of them executed [^rep].

He answered that the landlords had driven the peasants to it, and that the peasants "keep clear accounts" [^rep]. Then came his most famous lines:

> "a revolution is not a dinner party, or writing an essay, or painting a picture, or doing embroidery" [^rep]

A revolution, he went on, "is an insurrection, an act of violence by which one class overthrows another" [^rep]. Power built over centuries would not yield to good manners.

---

Who led the fight? Mao divided the peasants by class. **Rich peasants** held back, and **middle peasants** wavered. In Changsha County, the **poor peasants** made up 70 percent of the rural population, middle peasants 20 percent, and landlords and rich peasants 10 percent [^rep].

The poor were the most determined, because they had nothing to lose. Rich peasants mocked them:

> "You people have neither a tile over your heads nor a speck of land under your feet!" [^rep]

The poor peasants, Mao concluded, had always been the main force in the bitter fight in the countryside.

---

The movement struck at more than rent. Mao described four authorities that bound the Chinese peasant: political, clan, religious and, for women, the authority of husbands. They were "the four thick ropes binding the Chinese people, particularly the peasants" [^rep].

Once landlord power fell, the others began to totter. Women in Hengshan County marched into the clan temple and took seats at a banquet they had been barred from [^rep].

Yet Mao warned against smashing idols for the peasants. Let the peasants cast their idols aside "with their own hands" [^rep], when they were ready.

---

**The debate:** The party's leader in 1927 was Chen Duxiu, one of its founders [^chen1]. To keep the alliance with the Kuomintang, the leadership tried to check the peasants' 'excesses'. Mao's editors later blamed "the Right opportunists in the Party, headed by Chen Tu-hsiu" [^rep] (an older spelling of Chen Duxiu). Within months the Kuomintang turned on the Communists and the movement was crushed.

In 1929, writing on the side of the opposition led by Leon Trotsky, Stalin's opponent,, Chen accepted the blame but traced the line to Moscow: he had "sincerely executed the policy of opportunism of the international leaders" [^chen1]. Of the peasants, he admitted:

> "We did execute this shameful policy of checking the peasants' over-zealous action" [^chen2]
