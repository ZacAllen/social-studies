A month after *On Practice*, in August 1937, Mao delivered a second set of lectures in Yan'an (spelled Yenan in older translations). Their aim was the same: to overcome "the serious error of dogmatist thinking to be found in the Party" [^oc].

The subject was **dialectics**, the Marxist method of studying things in their change and their conflicts. Mao began with its core:

> "The law of contradiction in things, that is, the law of the unity of opposites, is the basic law of materialist dialectics." [^oc]

A **contradiction** here means two opposing sides within one thing, such as attack and defense, or bourgeoisie and proletariat.

---

Mao contrasted two **world outlooks**. The metaphysical outlook sees things as fixed, and explains change by outside forces. The dialectical outlook looks inside: "The fundamental cause of the development of a thing is not external but internal" [^oc].

Outside forces still matter, but they work through what is inside:

> "In a suitable temperature an egg changes into a chicken, but no temperature can change a stone into a chicken, because each has a different basis." [^oc]

The same held for nations. The Russian Revolution influenced China, but the changes it set off in China worked through China's own laws of development.

---

First, the **universality of contradiction**. Contradiction is present in everything, from start to finish. Engels wrote that "Motion itself is a contradiction" [^oc]. In war, attack and defense, advance and retreat, cannot exist without each other.

Mao drew the conclusion:

> "There is nothing that does not contain contradiction; without contradiction nothing would exist." [^oc]

He criticized the Soviet philosopher Deborin's school, which held that contradiction appears only at a later stage of a process. That, Mao argued, slipped back into explaining change from outside.

---

Universal truths are not enough. Each thing has its own **particular contradiction**, and dogmatists, Mao wrote, missed exactly this. So:

> "Qualitatively different contradictions can only be resolved by qualitatively different methods." [^oc]

The contradiction between proletariat and bourgeoisie is resolved by socialist revolution; between the masses and the feudal system, by democratic revolution; between colonies and imperialism, by national revolutionary war; within the Communist Party, by criticism and self-criticism [^oc].

Dogmatists erred, Mao wrote, because of their "subjectivist, one-sided and superficial way of looking at things" [^oc].

---

In any complex process, one contradiction leads. Mao called it the **principal contradiction**: it shapes all the others, and finding it is the key to the whole problem.

Usually, in China, it was between the feudal system and the masses. But when imperialism invades a semi-colonial country, its classes, except for some traitors, can temporarily unite in a national war:

> "At such a time, the contradiction between imperialism and the country concerned becomes the principal contradiction" [^oc]

Mao wrote this as Japan's full-scale invasion began. It was the theory behind the Communists' united front with the Kuomintang, their enemy of ten years.

---

Opposites also change places. Mao gave a striking image of how a contradiction becomes open conflict:

> "Before it explodes, a bomb is a single entity in which opposites coexist in given conditions." [^oc]

Class contradictions explode into revolution only at a certain stage. Not every contradiction must explode, though: "antagonism is one form, but not the only form, of the struggle of opposites" [^oc].

That mattered inside the party. Disagreements between right and wrong ideas need not become antagonistic, if those who erred corrected their mistakes. Hence, Mao wrote, "excessive struggle is obviously inappropriate" [^oc]. Twenty years later, he would build on this distinction to analyze socialist society.
