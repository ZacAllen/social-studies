On September 5, 1944, Zhang Side, a soldier in the Guards Regiment of the party's Central Committee, was making charcoal in the mountains of northern Shaanxi when the kiln collapsed and killed him. He had joined the revolution in 1933 and marched on the Long March [^stp].

Three days later, Mao spoke at his memorial meeting. The speech was short. It begins with the party and its armies:

> "These battalions of ours are wholly dedicated to the liberation of the people and work entirely in the people's interests." [^stp]

---

Mao turned to the meaning of death. He quoted Sima Qian, a Chinese historian of the second century BC, who wrote that death "may be weightier than Mount Tai or lighter than a feather" [^stp]. (Mount Tai is a famous mountain in China.)

Which, then, is which?

> "To die for the people is weightier than Mount Tai, but to work for the fascists and die for the exploiters and oppressors is lighter than a feather." [^stp]

Zhang Side, a soldier who died making charcoal, died for the people. His death, Mao said, was weightier than Mount Tai.

---

Serving the people also means accepting criticism from anyone:

> "Anyone, no matter who, may point out our shortcomings. If he is right, we will correct them." [^stp]

Mao's example was the slogan of "better troops and simpler administration" [^stp]. It came from Li Dingming, an enlightened landlord and not a Communist. The party adopted it because it benefited the people.

The test of an idea is the people's interest, not who proposed it.

---

The Communists already led base areas with 91 million people, Mao said, but that was not enough to liberate the whole nation [^stp]. Struggle meant sacrifice, but cadres must still avoid unnecessary deaths and care for every soldier:

> "all people in the revolutionary ranks must care for each other, must love and help each other." [^stp]

He proposed a rule: whenever anyone who had done useful work died, "be he soldier or cook" [^stp], there should be a funeral and a memorial meeting. In this way, the living would mourn the dead and unite.

---

The same ideal ran through two other short speeches Mao gave in these years. In 1939, he mourned **Norman Bethune**, a Canadian Communist surgeon who came to help China resist Japan and died of blood poisoning contracted while operating on wounded soldiers [^bethune].

Mao asked what spirit makes a foreigner adopt China's cause as his own. His answer was internationalism, and selflessness:

> "Comrade Bethune's spirit, his utter devotion to others without any thought of self, was shown in his great sense of responsibility in his work and his great warm-heartedness towards all comrades and the people." [^bethune]

---

In 1945, closing the party's Seventh Congress, Mao told a fable. An old man set out to dig away two mountains blocking his door. Mocked, he answered that his sons and grandsons would carry on the work [^foolish].

China's two mountains, Mao said, were **imperialism** and **feudalism**, and the party would dig until they were gone. In the fable, God sends angels to carry the mountains away. Mao gave the story his own ending:

> "Our God is none other than the masses of the Chinese people." [^foolish]

Serving the people, then, is not charity from above. It is the people themselves, organized, doing what no small group could do.
