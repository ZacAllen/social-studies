On June 22, 1941, Nazi Germany attacked the Soviet Union [^jul41]. Its aim was to turn Ukraine and Russia west of the Urals into a colony supplying food to Germany; its plans envisaged the deaths of up to thirty million people [^harrison]. Soviet people named the struggle that followed the **Great Patriotic War**.

Two years earlier, in 1939, the USSR had signed a non-aggression pact with Germany. Stalin defended it on his first wartime broadcast:

> "We secured our country peace for a year and a half and the opportunity of preparing our forces to repulse fascist Germany should she risk an attack on our country despite the pact." [^jul41]

---

That broadcast, on July 3, 1941, opened with "brothers and sisters" [^jul41]. Stalin called the whole people to a "patriotic war of liberation against the fascist enslavers" [^jul41].

He ordered that in any retreat, rolling stock and supplies be evacuated or destroyed:

> "the enemy must not be left a single engine, a single railway car, not a single pound of grain or gallon of fuel." [^jul41]

Behind German lines, guerrilla units were to blow up bridges and roads. Stalin framed the war as international: its aim was also to aid the peoples of Europe "groaning under the yoke of German fascism" [^jul41].

---

The first year was a disaster. By November 1941 the enemy stood "at the gates of Leningrad and Moscow", and the country had lost whole regions [^nov41].

On November 7, the anniversary of the October Revolution, Stalin addressed the Red Army on Red Square. He summoned the memory of Russian heroes such as Alexander Nevsky and Mikhail Kutuzov, and of Lenin [^nov41]:

> "The war you are waging is a war of liberation, a just war." [^nov41]

The losses of 1941 were catastrophic, and Stalin himself would later admit the government's errors in those years [^toast].

---

The turning point came on the Volga. On February 2, 1943, Stalin congratulated the Don Front on "the successful completion of the annihilation of the enemy forces surrounded at Stalingrad" [^don].

From then on, the Red Army drove west. On May 8, 1945, in Berlin, the German High Command signed the final act of surrender. Stalin addressed the country the next day:

> "The great day of victory over Germany has arrived." [^victory]

Hitler had planned to destroy Russia so that she could never rise again. Instead, Stalin said, Germany was "utterly defeated" [^victory].

---

The cost was staggering. The audit of Soviet population losses commissioned under Gorbachev put the war dead at 26–27 million, one in eight of the prewar population [^harrison]. Many died not in battle but of hunger, disease and exhaustion [^harrison].

For decades the true number was hidden. In 1946 Stalin announced losses of "a total of about seven million people" [^fulton]. Khrushchev later raised the official figure to 20 million, still a round number [^harrison]. Only the audit of the late Soviet years gave the real scale [^harrison].

A Marxist accounting starts from that truth, not from the comfortable figure.

---

Two weeks after the victory, at a Kremlin reception for Red Army commanders, Stalin spoke with unusual candor:

> "Our Government made not a few errors, we experienced at moments a desperate situation in 1941-1942" [^toast]

A different people, he said, might have told the government to go and made peace with Germany; instead they trusted it and made the sacrifices that ensured victory [^toast].

In 1941 Stalin had pointed out that Hitler's army had met serious resistance nowhere in Europe until it reached Soviet soil [^jul41]. The Soviet people broke it. That victory over fascism, and its terrible price, remains the defining memory of the Soviet experience.
