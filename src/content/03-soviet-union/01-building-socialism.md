After the October Revolution, the Bolsheviks ruled a vast agrarian country, surrounded by capitalist states that were far ahead of it in industry and arms [^fyp]. By the end of the 1920s, the party led by Joseph Stalin set out to change that at breakneck speed.

In 1931 Stalin told the managers of Soviet industry why the pace could not slacken. Old Russia, he said, had been beaten again and again because of her backwardness [^tbe]:

> "We are fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries. We must make good this distance in ten years. Either we do it, or we shall go under." [^tbe]

---

The answer was the **First Five-Year Plan** (1928–1932): a single state plan setting targets for the whole economy. In January 1933 Stalin reported that it had been fulfilled in four years and three months [^fyp]. He listed what the country had lacked before it:

> "We did not have an iron and steel industry, the basis for the industrialisation of the country." [^fyp]

Nor had it had tractor, automobile, machine-tool or aircraft industries. Industry's share of total output, he reported, rose from 48 percent in 1928 to 70 percent in 1932. Unemployment had been abolished, while 30 to 40 million were jobless in the capitalist countries [^fyp].

---

Industry needed grain, to feed the cities and to export in exchange for Western machinery [^free]. The party's answer was **collectivization**: merging millions of small peasant plots into large collective farms worked with tractors.

In 1929 Stalin hailed the middle peasants joining "by whole villages, volosts, districts, and even okrugs" [^gbreak] (volosts and okrugs were rural districts). He also announced a turn against the **kulaks**, the better-off peasants who hired labor: from restricting them to "eliminating the kulaks as a class" [^agr].

In practice it meant mass deportation. Several hundred thousand peasant families were deported in the early 1930s [^nlr214].

---

The pace outran the party's own rules. By February 20, 1930, Stalin reported, 50 percent of peasant farms had been collectivized [^dizzy]. In *Dizzy with Success* (March 1930) he condemned officials who threatened peasants with armed force or cut off their water and goods:

> "Collective farms must not be established by force." [^dizzy]

The coercion did not stop. Many peasants slaughtered their horses rather than hand them over, and draught animals fell by about 15 percent before tractors could replace them [^free]. By 1932 more than 60 percent of peasant farms were in collective farms [^fyp].

---

Then came catastrophe. Poor harvests in 1931 and 1932 met grain collection from the collective farms that was "brutally enforced" [^free]. Famine struck Ukraine, the Kuban, the Lower Volga and the North Caucasus [^free].

Estimates of the dead differ. The historian Stephen Wheatcroft put famine deaths at around 4.5 million; Robert Conquest estimated 7 million [^ucla]. A 2019 survey gives overall Soviet population losses by 1933 of 6–7 million, including about 3.5 million famine deaths in Ukraine [^free].

A separate famine in Kazakhstan, caused by forcing nomads to settle, killed a proportionally higher share of its people [^free].

---

How should socialists judge this? At the time, Stalin rejected the habit of blaming the peasants for the failures of grain procurement:

> "The peasants are not to blame at all. If we are to speak of responsibility and blame, then the responsibility falls wholly upon the Communists, and we Communists alone are to blame for all this." [^rural]

Yet the famine itself became a taboo topic in the Soviet era [^free]. Judged by the voluntary principle the party itself had proclaimed, the coercion of these years was a grave error [^dizzy], and the peasantry paid for it in millions of lives [^free].

---

The results shaped everything that followed. Stalin argued that without the new heavy industry the country "would remain unarmed while encircled by capitalist countries armed with modern technique" [^fyp]. That industry would soon face its hardest test in war.

The lessons that follow go deeper: Stalin's *Foundations of Leninism*, the theory behind building socialism in one country; the Great Patriotic War against Nazi Germany; and the repression and purges of the late 1930s, examined critically.
