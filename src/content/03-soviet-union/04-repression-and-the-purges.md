On December 1, 1934, the party leader Sergei Kirov was murdered. That evening, on Stalin's initiative, a directive ordered that cases of terrorism be sped up, and mass repression followed [^khr].

Stalin justified the hunt for hidden enemies by the danger facing one socialist country. In March 1937 he told the Central Committee that comrades had forgotten the threat of the capitalist states around them:

> "Capitalist encirclement-it is not an empty phrase, it is a very real and unpleasant phenomenon." [^mb]

Those states, he argued, would send wreckers, spies and killers into the Soviet rear [^mb]. The response became a catastrophe for the party and the country.

---

The **Great Purge** reached its peak in 1937–38. Secret police figures supplied to the leadership from 1953 onward give 681,692 executions in those two years; the historian R. W. Davies notes that they certainly omit some executions [^nlr219]. In his book *The Great Terror*, Robert Conquest had estimated one million executions [^nlr219].

The party itself was gutted. In 1956 the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev reported on the Central Committee elected at the 17th Congress in 1934:

> "of the 139 members and candidates of the Central Committee who were elected at the 17th Congress, 98 persons, i.e., 70 per cent, were arrested and shot" [^khr]

Of the congress's 1,966 delegates, 1,108 were arrested [^khr].

---

How was this possible? Stalin, Khrushchev said, originated the concept of the 'enemy of the people', which removed any need to prove a person's ideological errors [^khr]. The main proof of guilt became the accused's own confession, and "'confessions' were acquired through physical pressures against the accused" [^khr].

The army was struck too: many commanders and political workers were annihilated between 1937 and 1941, with grievous consequences when war began [^khr]. Writers, engineers and army officers were arrested in 1937–38, and a high proportion executed [^nlr214].

During the war, whole peoples were deported: the Karachai and Kalmyks in 1943, the Chechens and Ingush in March 1944, the Balkars in April 1944 [^khr]. Several million people from national minorities were deported in all [^nlr214].

---

How did the party reckon with this? The first admission came from Stalin himself, in his report to the 18th Congress in March 1939, speaking of the party purge of 1933–36:

> "It cannot be said that the purge was not accompanied by grave mistakes. There were unfortunately more mistakes than might have been expected." [^c18]

He still called the purge unavoidable and, on the whole, beneficial, and the condemned oppositionists "enemies of the people" [^c18]. The wave of mass arrests began to recede in 1939 [^khr].

---

The full reckoning came after Stalin's death. At the 20th Congress in February 1956, Khrushchev gave a speech denouncing Stalin's crimes and the cult of personality around him [^khr]. He described how innocent people had been condemned:

> "honest Communists were slandered, accusations against them were fabricated, and revolutionary legality was gravely undermined." [^khr]

He also attacked Stalin's thesis that class struggle must sharpen as socialism advances. Lenin, he argued, had used revolutionary violence while the exploiting classes were strong, not after they had been defeated [^khr]. For socialists, the lesson is plain: a workers' state must keep its own legality and inner-party democracy, or its weapons turn against its own people.

---

**The debate:** Was Stalin to be negated altogether? The Communist Party of China answered no in 1963, against Khrushchev's "complete negation" [^qst]. It named the errors directly:

> "in 1937 and 1938 there occurred the error of enlarging the scope of the suppression of counter-revolutionaries." [^qst]

It also found that he "did not fully apply proletarian democratic centralism and, to some extent, violated it" [^qst]. Yet it concluded that "his merits outweighed his faults" [^qst]. Khrushchev's line held that the cult of Stalin had caused grave harm the party had to root out [^khr]; the Chinese line held that Stalin's errors must be studied without discarding his whole legacy.
